You've shaped a beautiful loaf, chilled it overnight, and scored with confidence. But when you pull your bread from the oven, those careful cuts have barely opened. Instead of a dramatic ear or elegant pattern, you're left with flat, tight lines that look like they never happened.

This is one of the most frustrating sourdough problems because it feels like the final step—the one you can see—has failed you. But scoring that doesn't open is almost never about the scoring itself. It's a signal from your dough about what happened hours or even days before your blade touched the surface.

Let's look at exactly why this happens and how to achieve the bloom you're after.

## Cause 1: Overproofed Dough

### Why This Happens

When dough overproofs, the gluten network becomes exhausted and the gas bubbles inside have stretched to their limit. There's simply nothing left to give. During baking, the yeast has no more sugars to consume and the gluten has no more elasticity to expand. Your scores stay flat because the dough can't rise into them—it's already given everything it has.

Overproofed dough often feels pillowy and fragile. When you poke it, the indent stays rather than springing back slowly.

### How to Fix It

If you suspect overproofing before baking, you can try a cold rescue. Shape the dough gently (don't degas it aggressively) and place it in the refrigerator for 2-3 hours. The cold will slow fermentation and give the gluten a chance to firm up slightly. This won't fully reverse overproofing, but it can improve your results.

Score overproofed loaves more gently and at a shallower angle. Deep cuts on weak dough cause deflation.

### How to Prevent It

Learn to read your dough rather than the clock. Bulk fermentation should show a 50-75% rise with visible bubbles and a domed surface. The poke test during final proof should leave an indent that springs back slowly over 3-4 seconds. If it springs back instantly, it's underproofed. If the indent stays, you've gone too far.

In warm kitchens above 76°F (24°C), cut your bulk fermentation time by 30% or move the dough to a cooler spot.

## Cause 2: Underproofed Dough

### Why This Happens

This might seem contradictory—underproofed dough should have plenty of rise left, right? The problem is that underproofed dough is extremely tight. The gluten is wound up like a spring, and when you score it, the tension pulls the cuts back together almost immediately. The dough wants to close the wound rather than open it.

You'll notice underproofed loaves often crack unpredictably on the sides or bottom, because the expansion has to go somewhere and your scores aren't providing the release point.

### How to Fix It

If your dough feels very tight and resistant after cold retard, let it warm up at room temperature for 45-60 minutes before scoring. This allows the gluten to relax and gives the yeast a head start before the oven heat kills it.

Score underproofed dough deeper—about ½ inch—to give it a true path of least resistance.

### How to Prevent It

Don't rush bulk fermentation. At 75°F (24°C), expect 4-6 hours for most recipes using 20% levain. The dough should feel alive—jiggly, aerated, and noticeably lighter than when you started. After cold retard, the dough should hold a poke indent for a few seconds before slowly filling in about halfway.

## Cause 3: Dull Blade or Wrong Angle

### Why This Happens

A dull blade drags through the dough instead of slicing it cleanly. This dragging tears the gluten strands and creates a ragged edge that heals over during baking. The cuts seal themselves shut rather than opening.

Angle matters too. A vertical 90-degree cut creates two equal sides that push against each other. Neither side wins, so the score stays closed. For an ear to form, one side must be able to lift over the other.

### How to Fix It

Use a fresh razor blade or lame with a sharp edge. Curved blades work well for ears because they naturally create an angled cut. If using a straight blade, hold it at a 30-45 degree angle to the surface, almost parallel to the dough.

Score in one confident, swift motion. Going over the same line twice tears instead of cuts.

### How to Prevent It

Change your blade every 5-10 loaves, or more often if scoring high-hydration doughs. Keep blades dry to prevent rust. Before scoring, dip your blade in water—this helps it glide through the dough.

## Cause 4: Insufficient Steam

### Why This Happens

Steam keeps the crust soft and pliable during the first 15-20 minutes of baking. Without it, the outside of your loaf sets almost immediately, locking the scores in place before the bread has a chance to expand. The crust becomes a rigid shell that the interior pushes against instead of through.

You'll know steam was the issue if your crust looks matte and thick rather than glossy and crackled.

### How to Fix It

If you're using a Dutch oven, make sure it's properly preheated—at least 45 minutes at 500°F (260°C). The lid must fit tightly to trap moisture released by the dough. Bake covered for 20 minutes before removing the lid.

For open baking, add a cup of boiling water to a preheated cast iron pan on the oven floor immediately after loading your loaf. Add 2-3 ice cubes on top of the dough for extra surface moisture.

### How to Prevent It

Always preheat your Dutch oven with the lid on. Check that your oven temperature is accurate—an oven thermometer is a worthwhile investment. If your Dutch oven has a chipped enamel or warped lid, it may not seal properly.

## Cause 5: Weak Gluten Development

### Why This Happens

Weak gluten can't hold shape or support expansion. When you score dough with poor gluten structure, the cuts simply spread and flatten rather than opening with definition. The dough lacks the internal architecture to push upward through your scores.

You might notice this dough is slack, spreads sideways during proofing, and doesn't hold tension during shaping.

### How to Fix It

Add additional strength-building folds during bulk fermentation. Instead of 4 sets of stretch and folds, try 6 sets spaced 30 minutes apart. Coil folds are particularly effective for building strength in slack doughs.

Consider a longer autolyse—60-90 minutes—to allow the flour to fully hydrate before adding the levain and salt.

### How to Prevent It

Use bread flour with at least 12% protein content for reliable structure. If using whole grains, limit them to 20-30% of total flour until you're comfortable managing their effect on gluten development.

Don't skip the folds. They're not optional—they're how sourdough builds strength without kneading.

## Troubleshooting Quick Reference

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Quick Fix |
|--------|--------------|----------|
| Dough feels fragile, poke stays | Overproofed | Score shallow, bake immediately |
| Dough feels tight, springs back fast | Underproofed | Warm 45-60 min, score deeper |
| Ragged, torn score lines | Dull blade | Fresh blade, single swift motion |
| Matte, thick crust | Insufficient steam | Check Dutch oven seal, add steam |
| Dough spreads flat | Weak gluten | More folds, higher protein flour |

## The Path to Beautiful Scores

Dramatic scoring is the result of everything that came before—proper fermentation, adequate gluten development, precise proofing, and good steam. When all those elements align, your blade simply reveals what the dough was already prepared to do.

Work backward from flat scores. Ask what your dough felt like, how long it proofed, whether your blade was sharp, and if steam was present. The answer is always there in the process, waiting to be discovered.

Once you understand why scores open, you'll find that beautiful ears and patterns become reliable rather than random—and that's when sourdough baking becomes truly satisfying.
