You've probably seen recipes written in a strange format — 100% flour, 75% water, 20% starter, 2% salt. At first glance, it looks like the math doesn't add up. But once you understand baker's percentages, you'll wonder how you ever baked without them.

This simple system is the universal language of bread baking. It lets you scale recipes up or down effortlessly, compare different formulas at a glance, and understand the fundamental ratios that make great bread. Best of all, it's much easier than it looks.

## What Are Baker's Percentages?

Baker's percentages express every ingredient as a percentage of the total flour weight. Flour is always 100%, and everything else is calculated relative to it.

Here's a basic sourdough formula:

- **Flour:** 500g (100%)
- **Water:** 375g (75%)
- **Starter:** 100g (20%)
- **Salt:** 10g (2%)

To find each percentage, divide the ingredient weight by the flour weight and multiply by 100.

Water: 375 ÷ 500 × 100 = 75%

That's it. The percentages tell you the ratio of each ingredient to the flour, regardless of the total batch size.

## Why This System Matters

### Scale Any Recipe Instantly

Want to make two loaves instead of one? Or need to use up exactly 350g of flour? With baker's percentages, you just multiply.

If you have 350g flour and want 75% hydration:
350 × 0.75 = 262.5g water

No more awkward conversions or recipe hunting.

### Compare Recipes at a Glance

When you see one recipe at 65% hydration and another at 80%, you immediately know the second will be wetter and more challenging to handle. You can evaluate a recipe's difficulty before you commit to it.

### Understand What Makes Bread Work

Once you think in percentages, you start recognizing patterns. Most country loaves run 70-80% hydration. Enriched doughs might have 10-20% butter. Whole grain breads often need 5-10% more water than white flour breads. These patterns become intuitive.

## How to Calculate Baker's Percentages

### Converting a Recipe to Percentages

Take any recipe and identify the total flour weight first. If a recipe uses multiple flours, add them together — that combined weight is your 100%.

Example with mixed flours:
- Bread flour: 400g
- Whole wheat: 100g
- **Total flour: 500g (100%)**

Now calculate the rest:
- Water: 400g → 400 ÷ 500 × 100 = 80%
- Starter: 100g → 100 ÷ 500 × 100 = 20%
- Salt: 10g → 10 ÷ 500 × 100 = 2%

### Scaling a Recipe Up or Down

Start with your desired flour weight, then multiply by each percentage (as a decimal).

For a 750g flour batch at the same ratios:
- Flour: 750g (100%)
- Water: 750 × 0.80 = 600g
- Starter: 750 × 0.20 = 150g
- Salt: 750 × 0.02 = 15g

## A Note About Starter

Here's where it gets slightly more complex. Your sourdough starter contains both flour and water, usually in equal amounts (100% hydration starter). Technically, that flour and water contribute to your dough's total hydration.

For beginners, don't worry about this. Treat your starter as its own ingredient at 20% and move on. Your bread will be excellent.

As you advance, you can account for the flour and water in your starter for more precision. But this refinement matters most when you're developing your own formulas or need exact consistency across large batches.

## Common Hydration Ranges to Know

Understanding these ranges helps you choose recipes that match your skill level:

- **60-65%:** Stiff doughs, easy to handle, great for beginners
- **65-72%:** Classic country loaves, good balance of handling and openness
- **72-78%:** Moderately high hydration, more open crumb, requires confident handling
- **78-85%:** High hydration, very open crumb, challenging to shape
- **85%+:** Ciabatta territory, extremely wet, advanced technique required

## Common Mistakes

### Forgetting to Add Multiple Flours Together

If your recipe uses 300g bread flour and 200g whole wheat, your total flour is 500g. Each flour can also be expressed as a percentage of the total: 60% bread flour, 40% whole wheat.

### Confusing Total Percentages with Actual Percentages

Yes, the percentages add up to more than 100%. That's correct. Remember, everything is relative to flour — flour doesn't represent the total recipe, just the baseline.

### Over-Complicating Starter Calculations

Many bakers get stuck trying to account for the flour and water in their starter before they're comfortable with basic percentages. Master the simple version first.

## Practical Takeaways

1. **Flour is always 100%.** Everything else is calculated relative to it.

2. **Hydration percentage tells you difficulty.** Start around 70% and work your way up as your skills improve.

3. **Use a kitchen scale.** Baker's percentages only work with weight measurements. Volume measurements (cups) are too inconsistent.

4. **Write down your formulas.** When you bake something you love, record it in percentages. You'll be able to recreate it at any size forever.

5. **Don't stress about starter math.** At the beginner level, treating starter as a single ingredient works perfectly well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do baker's percentages add up to more than 100%?

Because they're not meant to represent the total recipe — they show each ingredient's relationship to the flour. Think of it as "for every 100 parts flour, I use 75 parts water." The flour is your constant reference point, not a fraction of the whole.

### How do I adjust hydration for whole grain flours?

Whole grain flours absorb more water than white flour. If you're substituting whole wheat for bread flour, start by adding 5% more water (so 75% becomes 80%). Adjust from there based on how your dough feels. Whole grains also benefit from longer autolyse times to fully hydrate.

### What percentage should my salt be?

Most bread formulas use 1.8-2.2% salt. Below 1.5%, bread tastes flat and fermentation can run too fast. Above 2.5%, the salt flavor becomes noticeable and can inhibit yeast activity. For most home bakers, 2% is the sweet spot — easy to remember and reliably good.

## Start Using Percentages Today

Next time you bake, write your recipe in percentages. Even if you don't scale it, you'll start seeing bread differently. You'll notice that your favorite country loaf and that bakery bread you love are probably built on similar ratios.

Baker's percentages aren't about making baking more complicated. They're about giving you a framework that makes every recipe more understandable, scalable, and repeatable. Once you speak this language, every bread formula becomes a conversation you can join.
