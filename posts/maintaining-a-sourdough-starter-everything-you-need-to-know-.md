Congratulations — you've created a sourdough starter. Now comes the real question: how do you keep this living thing alive without it taking over your life?

Good news. Maintaining a healthy starter is far simpler than most people make it. Once you understand what your starter actually needs (and what it doesn't), you'll spend just a few minutes a week keeping it happy and ready to bake.

## What Your Starter Actually Is

Your sourdough starter is a community of wild yeast and beneficial bacteria living together in a flour-and-water mixture. The yeast produces the carbon dioxide that makes your bread rise. The bacteria produce the acids that give sourdough its distinctive tang and help preserve your bread naturally.

This community is remarkably resilient. Starters have survived cross-country moves, weeks of neglect, and conditions that would kill most kitchen projects. Your job isn't to baby it — it's to provide regular meals and reasonable conditions.

## The Basics of Feeding

Feeding means discarding most of your starter and replacing it with fresh flour and water. This gives your yeast and bacteria new food to consume and prevents the mixture from becoming too acidic.

**A standard feeding ratio is 1:5:5**

That means 1 part starter, 5 parts flour, 5 parts water by weight.

For a typical home baker, this looks like:
- 20g mature starter
- 100g flour (all-purpose or bread flour works great)
- 100g water (room temperature)

Mix until no dry flour remains. Cover loosely and let it sit at room temperature.

## How Often to Feed

This depends entirely on where you store your starter.

**Room temperature (68-75°F / 20-24°C):** Feed once or twice daily. Your starter will be active and ready to use within 4-8 hours of feeding.

**Refrigerator (38-40°F / 3-4°C):** Feed once a week. The cold dramatically slows fermentation, so your starter enters a kind of hibernation between feedings.

Most home bakers keep their starter in the fridge and only bring it out when they're ready to bake. This approach is practical, economical, and produces excellent bread.

## The Weekly Refrigerator Routine

Here's what maintenance looks like for someone who bakes once or twice a week:

**Day before baking:**
1. Remove starter from fridge
2. Discard all but 20g
3. Feed with 100g flour and 100g water
4. Leave at room temperature for 4-8 hours until doubled and bubbly
5. Use what you need for your recipe
6. Return the rest to the fridge

**If you're not baking this week:**
1. Remove starter from fridge
2. Discard all but 20g
3. Feed with 100g flour and 100g water
4. Let it sit at room temperature for 1-2 hours (just until you see some activity)
5. Return to fridge

That's it. Five minutes a week keeps your starter healthy indefinitely.

## Signs of a Healthy Starter

A thriving starter will:
- Double (or more) in size within 4-8 hours of feeding at room temperature
- Have a pleasant, yeasty-tangy smell — like beer or yogurt
- Show lots of bubbles throughout, not just on top
- Have a slightly domed surface at its peak
- Pass the float test (a spoonful floats in water when it's ready)

## Signs Something's Off

**Dark liquid on top (hooch):** This grayish liquid means your starter is hungry. It's not harmful — just stir it in or pour it off and feed your starter. If this happens regularly, feed more frequently or increase your flour ratio.

**Sluggish rising:** If your starter takes much longer than usual to peak, it may need a few consecutive room-temperature feedings to rebuild strength. Feed it twice a day for 2-3 days.

**Off smells:** A healthy starter smells tangy, yeasty, almost fruity. If it smells like nail polish remover (acetone), it's very hungry and overly acidic. Several regular feedings will correct this.

**Mold:** Pink, orange, or fuzzy growth means contamination. Unfortunately, mold means starting over — but this is rare if you use clean utensils.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Using too much starter in your feeding ratio.** A 1:1:1 ratio (equal parts starter, flour, water) keeps things too acidic and can weaken your culture over time. Stick to 1:5:5 or even 1:10:10 for a stronger, more vigorous starter.

**Sealing the container airtight.** Your starter produces gas. A completely sealed container can build pressure or, more commonly, create an overly acidic environment. Use a loose lid, cloth cover, or jar with a slightly loose ring.

**Panicking over hooch or slow activity.** Starters are tough. A few extra feedings solve almost every problem. Don't throw it away at the first sign of trouble.

**Switching flours constantly.** Your starter adapts to what you feed it. Switching between very different flours (white to rye to whole wheat) can cause temporary sluggishness. Pick a flour and stick with it for regular feedings.

## What Flour Should You Use?

All-purpose or bread flour works beautifully for maintenance. These flours are consistent, affordable, and produce a reliably active starter.

Whole grain flours (whole wheat, rye) contain more wild yeast and nutrients, which can boost activity. Many bakers use a 50/50 mix of all-purpose and whole wheat for feedings.

Use whatever flour you can maintain consistently. Your starter will adapt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I skip a weekly feeding if my starter is in the fridge?**

Yes, occasionally. A healthy, well-established starter can go 2-3 weeks in the fridge without feeding. It will develop more hooch and need a couple of revival feedings before baking, but it won't die. That said, weekly feedings keep it in better shape and ready to bake with less advance planning.

**How do I know when my starter is ready to use for baking?**

Your starter is ready when it has doubled in size, is full of bubbles, and has a slightly domed top. This usually happens 4-8 hours after feeding at room temperature. The float test is a good indicator: drop a small spoonful into water. If it floats, your starter is active enough to leaven bread.

**What do I do with all the discard?**

Discard isn't waste — it's unfed starter that won't have the rising power for bread but still has wonderful flavor. Use it in pancakes, waffles, crackers, pizza dough, or quick breads. Store discard in a jar in the fridge and accumulate it throughout the week for discard recipes.

## Practical Takeaways

1. **Keep it simple.** A 1:5:5 feeding ratio once a week (refrigerated) or once daily (room temperature) is all most starters need.

2. **Trust the process.** Hooch, sluggishness, and other hiccups are normal and easily corrected with regular feedings. Don't abandon your starter at the first sign of trouble.

3. **Find your rhythm.** Match your feeding schedule to your baking schedule. Bake weekly? Feed weekly. Bake daily? Keep your starter at room temperature.

4. **Use your senses.** A healthy starter looks bubbly, smells pleasantly tangy, and doubles reliably. When something changes, a few extra feedings almost always restore balance.

Your starter isn't fragile — it's a survivor. Treat it like a low-maintenance houseplant rather than a needy pet, and it will reward you with beautiful bread for years to come.
