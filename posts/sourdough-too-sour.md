# Sourdough Too Sour? How to Control the Flavor of Your Bread

Sourdough flavor is one of the things you can actually control once you understand what drives it. Too sour, not sour enough — both are fixable, and both come down to the same variables: time, temperature, and starter condition.

## The Science in One Paragraph

Sourdough flavor comes from two acids produced during fermentation: **lactic acid** (mild, yoghurt-like) and **acetic acid** (sharp, vinegary). Cool, slow, long fermentation favors acetic acid — more sourness. Warm, fast fermentation favors lactic acid — milder flavor. Every variable you adjust either increases or decreases one of these two acids.

---

## Your Loaf Is Too Sour — Here's What to Change

### 1. Shorten or skip the cold proof
The overnight fridge retard is the single biggest driver of sourness. Bacteria keep working in the cold while yeast slows way down — the longer it sits cold, the more acetic acid builds up. Try baking after only 8 hours instead of 16, or skip the cold proof entirely and bake same-day.

### 2. Ferment warmer
Warmer temperatures favor lactic acid (milder) over acetic acid (sharp). Fermenting at 78–80°F instead of 68°F produces a noticeably milder loaf. Find a warm spot during bulk fermentation.

### 3. Use a fresher, more active starter
A starter used right at peak (just doubled, very active) produces a milder loaf than one used past peak. Let it peak and use it immediately rather than waiting until it starts to fall.

### 4. Increase starter percentage
Using 20–25% starter (by flour weight) shortens total fermentation time, which means less acid has time to build up. Less time fermenting = milder flavor.

### 5. Reduce whole wheat and rye content
Whole grain flours produce more acidic loaves. A 100% white flour loaf will always be milder than one with 30% whole wheat. If your bread is too sour, try reducing whole grain percentage.

---

## Your Loaf Isn't Sour Enough — Here's What to Change

### 1. Add an overnight cold proof
This is the most powerful single change you can make. Shape the dough and refrigerate it for 12–16 hours before baking. The long cold proof dramatically increases acetic acid production and flavor complexity.

### 2. Extend bulk fermentation
Ferment longer — let the dough develop more character before shaping. This works best in cooler kitchens where the extended time doesn't risk over-fermentation.

### 3. Use a smaller amount of starter
Dropping to 8–10% starter extends fermentation time naturally, giving acids more time to develop. Slower is more flavorful.

### 4. Use starter that's slightly past peak
A starter used just after it starts to fall back (past peak) is more acidic than one used at maximum rise. This transfers existing acids from the starter directly into the dough.

### 5. Add whole grain flour
Even 10–15% whole wheat or rye adds earthier, more complex flavor and increases overall acidity slightly.

---

## The One Variable That Changes Everything

If you do nothing else, add a cold overnight proof. Shape your dough, place it in the banneton, cover with plastic wrap, and refrigerate for 12–16 hours. Bake it cold, straight from the fridge.

The flavor difference between a room-temperature-proofed loaf and an overnight cold-proofed loaf is remarkable. Most of the sourdough you've eaten at a good bakery spent a long time cold. Now you know why.
