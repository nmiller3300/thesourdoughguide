You pull your loaf from the oven expecting that beautiful, symmetrical rise—and instead find something that looks like it's trying to escape sideways. One side has ballooned up dramatically while the other stayed flat, or the whole loaf has tilted at an angle that makes slicing a challenge.

A lopsided sourdough isn't just an aesthetic problem. It's your bread telling you something went wrong during shaping, scoring, proofing, or baking. The good news? Once you understand what causes uneven expansion, you can fix it easily.

Let's break down every reason your sourdough might be baking lopsided—and exactly how to get that symmetrical, proud rise you're after.

## Cause #1: Uneven Shaping and Surface Tension

### Why This Happens

When you shape your loaf, you're creating a skin of surface tension that holds everything together during the final proof and oven spring. If one side has significantly more tension than the other, the weaker side will give way first when heat hits the dough. Gas takes the path of least resistance, so your loaf expands wherever the structure is weakest.

This often happens when you pull the dough more tightly on one side during shaping, or when you don't seal the bottom seam evenly.

### How to Fix It

Reshape your loaf if you notice uneven tension before it goes into the banneton. Don't be afraid to start over—a 5-minute fix now prevents a lopsided loaf later.

Focus on creating even tension across the entire surface. When shaping a boule, pull the dough toward you from all four directions equally. For a batard, make sure your letter folds and final roll create consistent tightness from end to end.

### How to Prevent It

Practice your shaping on a clean counter without flour first—this helps you feel the tension building. Work in a methodical pattern: if you're shaping a boule, think of it like tightening opposite lug nuts on a tire. Pull from the top, then the bottom, then left, then right. Repeat until the surface is smooth and taut all around.

When you flip the shaped dough into your banneton, place it seam-side up and make sure the seam runs straight down the center, not off to one side.

## Cause #2: Off-Center or Asymmetrical Scoring

### Why This Happens

Your score marks tell the bread where to expand. If you score off-center or at an uneven angle, you're essentially telling one side of the loaf "this is where you should open up" while the other side has no clear release point.

A score that's too deep on one side and shallow on the other creates the same problem. The deeper cut opens more dramatically, while the shallow side either stays closed or tears unpredictably.

### How to Fix It

If you've already scored and notice it's uneven, you can add a small corrective score on the neglected side before loading into the oven. It won't be perfect, but it helps balance the expansion.

### How to Prevent It

Before you score, take a moment to visualize where you want the bread to open. For a single score down the center of a batard, position your lame at the top of the loaf and draw a mental line straight down the middle.

Hold your lame at a consistent 30-45 degree angle and use one confident stroke. Hesitation creates uneven depth. The cut should be about 1/4 to 1/2 inch deep—consistent from start to finish.

For boules with a box score or cross pattern, use the center of the loaf as your reference point and keep your lines equidistant from the edges.

## Cause #3: Uneven Proofing

### Why This Happens

During the final proof, if one side of your dough is warmer than the other, that side will ferment faster and develop more gas. When the loaf hits the oven, the more-proofed side has already exhausted much of its rising potential, while the under-proofed side still has energy to expand.

This commonly happens when your banneton sits near a warm appliance, by a sunny window, or in a spot with a draft on one side.

### How to Fix It

If you suspect uneven proofing, rotate your banneton 180 degrees halfway through the final proof. This won't completely fix the problem, but it helps even things out.

### How to Prevent It

Proof your dough in a location with consistent temperature on all sides. Inside your oven with just the light on (not the heat) works beautifully—the enclosed space maintains even warmth. A proofing box is even better.

If you're cold-proofing in the refrigerator, place your banneton in the center of a shelf, not against the back wall where it's coldest.

## Cause #4: Uneven Oven Heat

### Why This Happens

Most home ovens have hot spots. If your Dutch oven sits in a position where one side receives significantly more heat than the other, that side will experience faster oven spring. The crust sets unevenly, and you end up with a tilted loaf.

### How to Fix It

If you notice your loaves consistently lean the same direction, rotate your Dutch oven 180 degrees about 20 minutes into the bake—after you've removed the lid and the crust has set enough to handle movement.

### How to Prevent It

Preheat your Dutch oven for a full 45-60 minutes at 500°F (260°C) before loading your dough. This ensures the pot itself has even heat distribution throughout.

Position your Dutch oven in the center of the oven, on a middle rack. Avoid placing it too close to the heating element.

Consider getting an oven thermometer and testing different positions to find where your oven heats most evenly.

## Cause #5: Dough Loaded Off-Center in the Dutch Oven

### Why This Happens

This one's simple but surprisingly common. If your dough lands closer to one side of the Dutch oven when you flip it in, that side has less room to expand. The bread grows toward the open space, creating a lopsided final shape.

### How to Fix It

If you notice your dough has landed off-center immediately after loading, you have about 5 seconds to gently nudge it into position before the bottom begins to set. Use a bench scraper or silicone spatula—work quickly and don't overthink it.

### How to Prevent It

Practice your flip. If you're turning the dough out of a banneton directly into the Dutch oven, position the banneton directly over the center of the pot before inverting.

Alternatively, flip onto parchment paper first, then use the parchment as a sling to lower the dough into the center of the pot. This gives you much more control and virtually eliminates off-center placement.

## A Quick Diagnostic Checklist

Next time you pull out a lopsided loaf, ask yourself these questions:

1. Did the loaf expand more where I scored it? → Scoring placement issue
2. Did it bulge from the side or bottom? → Shaping tension issue
3. Does one side look more baked than the other? → Oven heat distribution issue
4. Was my dough touching one wall of the Dutch oven? → Loading position issue
5. Did one side of my kitchen feel warmer during proofing? → Proofing environment issue

## The Path to Symmetry

A lopsided loaf is still delicious bread—it just doesn't look quite as impressive on your cutting board or Instagram feed. But more importantly, fixing the lopsided problem often fixes other issues too, because the same attention to even tension, centered scoring, and consistent fermentation that creates a symmetrical loaf also creates better oven spring, more open crumb, and a more predictable bake overall.

Start with shaping—that's where most lopsided loaves originate. Take an extra 30 seconds to check your tension is even all the way around, position your scores deliberately, and load your dough centered in the pot.

Your next loaf will stand tall and proud, exactly the way you shaped it.
