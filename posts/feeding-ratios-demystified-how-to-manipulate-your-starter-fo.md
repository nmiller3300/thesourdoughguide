Every sourdough baker eventually faces the same frustration: your starter peaks too early, too late, or not predictably at all. You've been feeding it the same amount every day, yet somehow it refuses to cooperate with your schedule.

The solution isn't luck or magic. It's understanding feeding ratios—the mathematical relationship between your existing starter, fresh flour, and water. Once you master this, you control your starter instead of it controlling you.

## What Feeding Ratios Actually Mean

A feeding ratio expresses the proportion of mature starter to fresh flour to water. When someone says they use a 1:5:5 ratio, they mean one part starter, five parts flour, five parts water by weight.

So if you're using 20g of mature starter:
- 1:5:5 = 20g starter + 100g flour + 100g water
- 1:2:2 = 20g starter + 40g flour + 40g water
- 1:10:10 = 20g starter + 200g flour + 200g water

The first number (starter amount) determines how much active microbial population you're carrying forward. The second and third numbers (flour and water) determine how much fresh food you're providing.

## The Science Behind the Ratio

Your starter contains billions of wild yeast cells and lactic acid bacteria competing for the same food source: the starches and sugars in flour. When you feed your starter, you're essentially diluting this population and giving them fresh fuel.

**Lower ratios (1:1:1 or 1:2:2)** mean you're carrying forward a larger microbial population relative to available food. The organisms consume the flour quickly, and your starter peaks fast—often within 4-6 hours at room temperature. But it also crashes fast, becoming acidic and potentially weakening as the population exhausts its food supply.

**Higher ratios (1:5:5 or 1:10:10)** dilute the microbial population significantly. Fewer organisms means slower initial activity, but there's abundant food. Your starter takes longer to peak—8-12 hours or even longer—but maintains that peak for a wider window before declining.

Temperature amplifies everything. At 78°F (26°C), a 1:5:5 feeding might peak in 8 hours. At 65°F (18°C), the same ratio could take 16 hours.

## How to Choose Your Ratio

### For Morning Bakes
If you want to mix dough first thing in the morning, feed your starter the night before with a 1:5:5 ratio using cool water (around 70°F/21°C). An 8pm feeding typically peaks around 6-8am, depending on your kitchen temperature.

### For Evening Bakes
Feed your starter in the morning with a 1:3:3 or 1:4:4 ratio. At average room temperature (72-75°F), expect a peak in 6-8 hours.

### For Weekend Baking with Weekday Neglect
If you can only tend to your starter once daily, use 1:10:10 or even 1:15:15 ratios. This slows fermentation enough that your starter won't over-ferment before you return 24 hours later. Combine with cooler temperatures for even more buffer.

### For Hot Summer Kitchens
When ambient temperature climbs above 80°F (27°C), increase your ratio to 1:8:8 or higher. The extra flour compensates for accelerated fermentation. Without adjustment, your starter will peak and collapse while you're at work.

### For Cold Winter Kitchens
Drop to 1:2:2 or 1:3:3 when temperatures hover around 65°F (18°C). Otherwise, your starter may never fully peak before your baking window.

## Step-by-Step: Calibrating Your Personal Ratio

Every starter behaves differently based on its unique microbial culture, your flour, and your environment. Here's how to find your ideal ratio:

**Day 1: Establish a Baseline**

1. Feed your starter at 1:5:5 using 20g starter, 100g flour, 100g water at 78°F (26°C).
2. Mark the starting level with a rubber band.
3. Check every 2 hours, noting the time it doubles, triples, and peaks.
4. Record when it begins falling.

**Day 2: Test a Lower Ratio**

1. Feed at 1:3:3 using 30g starter, 90g flour, 90g water.
2. Repeat the observation process.

**Day 3: Test a Higher Ratio**

1. Feed at 1:8:8 using 12g starter, 96g flour, 96g water.
2. Observe and record.

After three days, you'll understand how ratio changes affect timing. Most bakers settle on 2-3 ratios they rotate depending on their schedule.

## The Float Test Myth

Many bakers rely on the float test—dropping a spoonful of starter in water to see if it floats—to determine readiness. While useful, it's not the full picture.

A starter floats when it contains enough gas bubbles, which happens before, during, and slightly after peak. But a starter that's beginning to collapse will still float while losing fermentation power.

Better indicators:
- Volume has increased 2-3x from feeding
- Domed top (flat or concave means it's past peak)
- Bubbles throughout, not just on top
- Smells pleasantly tangy, not sharply acidic

## Flour Type Matters Too

Different flours ferment at different speeds:

**Whole wheat and rye** contain more wild yeast, more minerals, and more accessible sugars. A 1:5:5 feeding with whole grain flour peaks significantly faster than the same ratio with white bread flour. If using whole grains, increase to 1:7:7 or 1:8:8 to compensate.

**Bread flour** provides steady, predictable fermentation. It's the standard baseline for most ratio discussions.

**All-purpose flour** ferments slightly slower due to lower protein and mineral content. Consider dropping your ratio by one step (1:4:4 instead of 1:5:5).

## Common Mistakes

**Using volume instead of weight.** A tablespoon of starter varies wildly depending on how bubbly it is. Always measure by weight for consistent results.

**Ignoring temperature.** A 1:5:5 ratio at 80°F behaves like 1:3:3 at 72°F. Ratio and temperature work together—you can't dial in one without considering the other.

**Never adjusting.** Seasons change. Your kitchen temperature fluctuates. A ratio that worked in January might fail in July. Stay observant and adapt.

**Overcomplicating things.** You don't need five different ratios. Most bakers thrive with two: a standard ratio for typical bakes and a high ratio for slow fermentation or hot weather.

**Feeding a hungry starter at high ratio.** If your starter has been neglected and is very acidic, a 1:10:10 feeding may not contain enough active organisms to fully colonize all that flour. Rebuild with 1:2:2 for a few feeds first, then transition to higher ratios.

## Building Your Levain with Ratios

The same principles apply when building a levain. If your recipe calls for 100g of levain and you're mixing dough in 8 hours, use:

- 10g starter + 50g flour + 50g water (1:5:5, yields 110g)

If you need it ready in 4-5 hours:
- 20g starter + 50g flour + 50g water (roughly 1:2.5:2.5)

If you're building overnight for a morning mix (12+ hours):
- 5g starter + 50g flour + 50g water (1:10:10)

## The Freedom of Understanding

Once you internalize feeding ratios, you stop being a slave to your starter's schedule. Dinner plans changed? Adjust tomorrow's feeding ratio. Want to bake bread after work instead of before? Shift your ratio higher. Heatwave rolling through? Compensate accordingly.

This is the difference between following recipes and truly understanding fermentation. Your starter becomes a tool that responds to your life, not the other way around.

Start with 1:5:5 as your baseline. Observe obsessively for a week. Then begin adjusting, one variable at a time. Within a month, you'll instinctively know which ratio fits your schedule—and your bread will be more consistent than ever.
