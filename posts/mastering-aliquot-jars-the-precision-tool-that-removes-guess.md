For years, I relied on a combination of experience, intuition, and hope to judge when bulk fermentation was complete. Sometimes I nailed it. Other times, I pulled my dough too early or let it go too long. The results were inconsistent, and the learning curve felt unnecessarily steep.

Then I started using an aliquot jar, and everything changed.

This simple technique—taking a small sample of your dough and fermenting it in a straight-sided container—gives you something invaluable: **measurable rise percentage**. Instead of poking, hoping, and second-guessing, you watch a clear visual indicator that tells you exactly where your dough is in fermentation.

## The Science Behind the Aliquot Jar

During bulk fermentation, wild yeast produces carbon dioxide as it consumes sugars in your flour. This gas gets trapped in the gluten network, causing your dough to expand. The rate and extent of this expansion depend on multiple factors: starter activity, ambient temperature, hydration level, flour composition, and salt content.

The problem? It's nearly impossible to accurately gauge rise percentage in a large, irregular mass of dough sitting in a bowl or container. The dough spreads, domes, and shifts in ways that obscure true volume change.

An aliquot jar solves this by isolating a small portion of dough in a **tall, narrow, straight-sided container** where rise is immediately visible and measurable. Because this sample comes from your actual dough (after all ingredients are incorporated), it ferments at exactly the same rate as your main batch.

When your aliquot shows 50% rise, your main dough has risen 50%. When it hits 75%, so has everything else. The correlation is remarkably precise.

## What You'll Need

- A small, straight-sided glass jar (4 oz to 8 oz works perfectly)
- A rubber band or dry-erase marker
- Your mixed dough, after all additions are complete

I prefer small mason jars or tall, narrow spice jars. The key is **straight sides**—tapered containers distort your readings because the same volume increase looks different at different heights.

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Your Aliquot Jar

### Step 1: Complete Your Mixing

Finish incorporating all ingredients into your dough. If you're using bassinage, add that water. If you're including salt after autolyse, mix it in fully. The aliquot must represent your final dough composition.

### Step 2: Take Your Sample

Pinch off approximately **50 to 75 grams** of dough. This is enough to see clearly without wasting significant dough.

### Step 3: Pack It Into the Jar

Press the dough firmly into the bottom of your jar, eliminating air pockets. Use wet fingers or a small spatula to pack it down and create a **flat, even surface**. This flat top is critical—an uneven surface makes tracking rise nearly impossible.

### Step 4: Mark the Starting Level

Place a rubber band around the jar at exactly the top of your dough, or use a dry-erase marker to draw a line. This is your baseline.

### Step 5: Ferment Together

Keep the aliquot jar in the same location as your main dough. Same temperature, same environment. If your dough is covered, loosely cover the jar too (don't seal it airtight—gas needs somewhere to go).

### Step 6: Track the Rise

Check your aliquot periodically. For most sourdough at room temperature, check every 30 to 45 minutes after the first hour.

## Understanding Rise Percentages

Different rise targets serve different purposes:

**25-40% rise:** Early bulk. Gluten is developing, fermentation is active, but you're nowhere near done. Continue with folds if your schedule calls for them.

**50-75% rise:** The sweet spot for most sourdough. At this range, fermentation has produced enough gas for good oven spring, but the dough retains enough food and structural integrity to continue rising after shaping and during baking. **Most bakers end bulk here.**

**75-100% rise:** Pushing the limits. This works beautifully for same-day bakes where you want maximum flavor development and are heading straight to the oven after shaping. However, if you're cold retarding, starting at this level risks over-fermentation.

**Over 100% rise:** Danger zone. Your dough has likely exhausted much of its available food, and the gluten network may be weakening. Over-proofed loaves spread flat, lack oven spring, and often have gummy, dense crumb.

## Calibrating for Your Process

Here's the critical insight: **the ideal rise percentage depends on your complete process.**

If you're doing a long cold retard (12+ hours), end bulk around **50-60%** rise. Fermentation slows dramatically in the refrigerator but doesn't stop completely. Starting with more headroom prevents over-proofing.

If you're baking same-day with a short room-temperature proof, you can push bulk to **75-80%** rise because there's less time for additional fermentation before the oven.

If you're using a very active starter in a warm kitchen, fermentation accelerates. You'll hit your target rise faster, so check your aliquot more frequently.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

### Mistake 1: Taking the Sample Too Early

If you take your aliquot before adding salt, your sample will ferment faster than your main dough (salt slows fermentation by approximately 10-15%). Always take your sample after all ingredients are fully incorporated.

### Mistake 2: Uneven Starting Surface

A domed or cratered starting surface makes accurate measurement impossible. Take the extra 30 seconds to press the dough flat and smooth.

### Mistake 3: Different Temperatures

If your aliquot sits on a cold counter while your main dough is in a warm proofing box, they'll ferment at different rates. Keep them together, always.

### Mistake 4: Obsessing Over Exact Percentages

The aliquot is a guide, not a dictator. If your dough looks and feels ready at 55% rise, trust your judgment. If it seems sluggish at 70%, give it more time. The jar provides data; you make decisions.

### Mistake 5: Forgetting to Adjust for Your Schedule

A 75% rise might be perfect for one process and disastrous for another. Always consider what comes next: shaping time, proof length, retard duration, and ambient conditions all matter.

## When to Use an Aliquot Jar

**Learning a new formula:** When you're unfamiliar with how a specific flour blend or hydration level behaves, the aliquot removes guesswork.

**Temperature fluctuations:** If your kitchen temperature varies seasonally or throughout the day, the aliquot accounts for this automatically by fermenting under the same conditions.

**Training your eye:** Use the aliquot alongside visual cues in your main dough. Over time, you'll learn what 50% rise looks like in your container, and you may not need the jar anymore.

**High-stakes bakes:** When consistency matters—feeding a crowd, testing a new recipe, or baking for an event—the aliquot provides insurance.

## Making It Routine

After a few bakes, setting up an aliquot jar takes under a minute and becomes automatic. The insight it provides is worth far more than the effort.

I still use one almost every bake. Not because I can't judge fermentation without it, but because I want data. I want to correlate what I see in the jar with what I observe in the dough. I want to know, not guess.

That knowledge compounds. Every bake teaches you something concrete. And over time, you develop the kind of intuition that seems like magic to beginners—but is really just accumulated observation, made precise by a simple glass jar and a rubber band.
