You've done everything right. Your dough is beautifully proofed, your scoring is on point, and you can smell that incredible sourdough aroma filling your kitchen. Then comes the moment of truth — and your loaf won't budge. The bottom is welded to the Dutch oven, and you're left scraping and prying, watching your gorgeous crust tear apart.

This is one of the most frustrating sourdough problems because it happens at the very end, after all your hard work. But here's the good news: bread sticking to your Dutch oven is entirely preventable once you understand what's going wrong.

## Cause #1: Insufficient Barrier Between Dough and Pot

### Why This Happens

Cast iron and enameled cast iron, while excellent at heat retention, create a direct contact surface that can grip your dough during baking. The natural sugars in your bread caramelize against the hot metal, essentially creating a bond. Without something between your dough and the pot, you're relying on luck.

### How to Fix It

Your best options are parchment paper or a combination approach:

**Parchment paper method:** Cut a square of parchment roughly 12-14 inches. After scoring your loaf, lift it into the Dutch oven using the parchment as a sling. The paper can extend up the sides — it won't burn at typical baking temperatures of 450-500°F (232-260°C).

**Semolina or rice flour method:** If you prefer baking directly on cast iron, dust the bottom generously with coarse semolina or rice flour — about 2 tablespoons. These don't absorb moisture like regular flour and create tiny ball-bearing-like particles that prevent sticking.

**Combination approach:** Use parchment AND dust it with semolina. This is my foolproof method for high-hydration doughs that tend to spread and seep.

### How to Prevent It

Always have your barrier ready before you turn out your proofed dough. Prep your parchment while your Dutch oven preheats. Make it part of your routine, not an afterthought.

## Cause #2: Your Dutch Oven Isn't Hot Enough

### Why This Happens

This seems counterintuitive, but a properly heated Dutch oven actually releases bread better than a lukewarm one. When the pot is screaming hot (at least 450°F/232°C), the bottom of your dough immediately begins to set and crust over. This rapid crust formation creates a natural barrier.

When the pot isn't hot enough, the dough sits there slowly oozing moisture into contact with the metal, creating that stubborn bond.

### How to Fix It

Preheat your Dutch oven for a full 45-60 minutes at 500°F (260°C). Yes, that seems excessive. Yes, it matters. An instant-read thermometer touching the bottom of your pot should register at least 450°F (232°C).

If your oven runs cool (many do), add another 15 minutes of preheating.

### How to Prevent It

Invest in an oven thermometer to know your actual oven temperature. Preheat longer than you think necessary, especially in winter when kitchens run cooler and cast iron takes longer to heat through.

## Cause #3: High-Hydration Dough Seeping Underneath

### Why This Happens

If you're baking at 78% hydration or higher, your dough contains more free water. When you place it in the Dutch oven, some of that moisture can seep out the bottom, creating a wet layer that bakes into a sticky, gummy mess that bonds with the pot.

### How to Fix It

For high-hydration loaves, never skip the parchment paper. Additionally, give your shaped dough a slightly longer final proof in the banneton to allow the surface to dry out just a bit — about 10-15 minutes extra at room temperature before refrigerating for cold retard.

When you turn out your dough, let it sit for 30 seconds on the parchment before lowering it into the hot pot. This brief rest allows any moisture on the bottom to begin evaporating.

### How to Prevent It

Dust your bannetons more generously with rice flour. Before inverting your dough, make sure the surface that will become the bottom isn't wet or tacky. If it is, gently dust it with rice flour before placing on parchment.

## Cause #4: Damaged or Improperly Seasoned Cast Iron

### Why This Happens

Bare cast iron Dutch ovens rely on their seasoning — a layer of polymerized oil — to provide a somewhat non-stick surface. If that seasoning is damaged, patchy, or was never properly built up, your bread will stick.

Even enameled cast iron can become problematic if the enamel is chipped or stained with carbonized residue that creates an uneven surface.

### How to Fix It

**For bare cast iron:** Re-season your Dutch oven. Wash with hot water and a stiff brush, dry completely over low heat, apply a thin layer of flaxseed or vegetable oil, and bake upside down at 450°F (232°C) for one hour. Repeat 2-3 times.

**For enameled cast iron:** Clean thoroughly with baking soda paste to remove any carbonized buildup. If the enamel is chipped, always use parchment paper as your protective barrier.

### How to Prevent It

Never soak cast iron in water. Clean while still warm but not hot. Store with the lid slightly ajar to prevent moisture buildup. For enameled versions, avoid thermal shock — don't put a cold pot in a hot oven.

## Cause #5: Sugary Additions in Your Dough

### Why This Happens

If you're baking an enriched sourdough with honey, maple syrup, or added sugars, these caramelize faster and more aggressively than the natural sugars in plain dough. They essentially become glue at high temperatures.

### How to Fix It

For any enriched dough, parchment paper is non-negotiable. Additionally, consider lowering your baking temperature by 25°F (about 14°C) and extending your baking time by 5-8 minutes to achieve proper browning without aggressive caramelization.

### How to Prevent It

Know your dough. Any time you add sweeteners, dried fruits, or other sugar-containing inclusions, adjust your approach accordingly.

## The Perfect Release System

Here's my foolproof routine:

1. Preheat Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 50 minutes minimum
2. Cut parchment to size while it preheats
3. Dust parchment lightly with semolina
4. Turn out proofed dough onto parchment
5. Score immediately
6. Lift into Dutch oven using parchment as handles
7. Bake covered for 20 minutes, then remove lid
8. Continue baking until internal temperature reaches 205-210°F (96-99°C)

When you remove the lid after those first 20 minutes, you'll already see the parchment pulling away from the sides. That's how you know you've won.

Your beautifully crafted loaf deserves a clean release. With these adjustments, you'll never scrape another crust off the bottom of your Dutch oven again.
