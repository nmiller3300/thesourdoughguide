You pulled a gorgeous loaf from the oven. The crust crackled as it cooled, singing that beautiful song every baker loves. But a few hours later, you reached for a slice and found... a soft, almost leathery crust that lost all its crunch.

This is one of the most frustrating sourdough problems because you did everything right during baking. The disappointment of a soggy crust after all that work is real.

The good news? This is completely fixable. Let's walk through exactly why your crust softens and how to keep that satisfying shatter for days.

## Understanding What Makes Crust Crispy

Before we troubleshoot, it helps to understand what's actually happening. A crispy crust is essentially dehydrated. During baking, moisture escapes from the outer layer of bread, creating that thin, brittle shell we love.

But here's the thing: your bread's interior is still full of moisture. After baking, that moisture slowly migrates outward, rehydrating the crust from the inside. This process is called "moisture migration," and it's the enemy of crispy crusts.

Your job is to slow this migration and give that moisture somewhere to go besides your crust.

## Cause #1: Cooling in a Humid Environment

### Why This Happens

If your kitchen is humid—whether from weather, cooking, or running a dishwasher—that ambient moisture gets absorbed by your cooling crust. Bread crust is hygroscopic, meaning it attracts and holds water from the air. In a humid kitchen, your crust doesn't stand a chance.

### How to Fix It

Move your bread to the driest spot in your home while it cools. Near an open window (if it's dry outside), in an air-conditioned room, or even near a fan works well. You want air circulation and low humidity.

If you live in a consistently humid climate, consider cooling your bread in the oven with the door cracked open. Turn the oven off, crack the door about 4 inches, and let the bread cool in that dry environment for 30-45 minutes before removing it.

### How to Prevent It

Make bread on dry days when possible, or create a drier microenvironment. A cooling rack placed near a dehumidifier or in an air-conditioned room makes a significant difference.

## Cause #2: Storing Bread Too Soon

### Why This Happens

When you seal bread in a bag or container before it's completely cooled, you trap residual heat and moisture. That trapped steam has nowhere to go except right back into your crust. Even bread that feels cool on the outside can still be releasing moisture from its core.

### How to Fix It

Always cool your bread completely on a wire rack—this means a minimum of 2 hours for a standard 900g loaf, ideally 4 hours. I know waiting is hard when your kitchen smells incredible, but patience here pays off.

To test if bread is ready for storage, place your palm flat against the bottom of the loaf. It should feel completely room temperature, with no warmth whatsoever.

### How to Prevent It

Build cooling time into your baking schedule. If you bake in the afternoon, don't store until evening. If you bake at night, leave the bread on the counter overnight (covered loosely with a clean kitchen towel) and store it in the morning.

## Cause #3: Improper Storage Method

### Why This Happens

Plastic bags and airtight containers are the fastest way to soften a crust. They trap the moisture that naturally escapes from bread, creating a humid microenvironment right against your crust. Within hours, that trapped moisture turns your crispy shell soft and almost rubbery.

### How to Fix It

Store your bread cut-side down on a wooden cutting board, or wrap it in a clean linen or cotton cloth. These materials allow moisture to escape gradually while still protecting the bread from drying out completely.

For longer storage (2-3 days), a paper bag works well. It's breathable enough to let moisture escape but provides some protection. Place the bread cut-side down inside, fold the top loosely, and store at room temperature.

### How to Prevent It

Invest in a bread box with ventilation, or use a dedicated bread bag made from linen or cotton. These are designed specifically to balance moisture release with freshness. Avoid plastic entirely for the first 24 hours after baking.

## Cause #4: Underbaked Interior

### Why This Happens

If your bread's interior isn't fully baked, it contains more moisture than it should. All that excess moisture has to go somewhere, and it heads straight for your crust. An underbaked loaf might look perfect on the outside but still be slightly gummy inside—and that extra moisture will soften your crust faster than anything else.

### How to Fix It

Bake longer than you think you need to. Most sourdough benefits from an internal temperature of 205-210°F (96-99°C). Use an instant-read thermometer inserted into the center of the loaf to check.

Also, don't be afraid of dark color. A deep golden-brown to mahogany crust typically indicates a well-baked interior. If your bread looks pale golden, it's probably underbaked.

### How to Prevent It

After removing the lid from your Dutch oven (usually around the 20-minute mark), continue baking for at least 20-25 more minutes at 450°F (230°C). For my standard loaves, total bake time is 45-50 minutes. Trust the thermometer over appearance.

## Cause #5: Not Enough Final Crust Development

### Why This Happens

Removing bread from the oven the moment it looks done doesn't give the crust enough time to fully dehydrate. A thin, underdeveloped crust softens much faster than a thick, well-caramelized one.

### How to Fix It

For maximum crust crispness, turn off the oven when your bread reaches the right internal temperature, crack the door open, and let the bread sit inside for an additional 10-15 minutes. This extra time in the dry, residual heat drives out more moisture from the crust without overbaking the interior.

### How to Prevent It

Always plan for this resting period. When your timer goes off, check the internal temperature—if it's at 205-210°F, turn off the oven, crack the door, and walk away for 10 minutes.

## Quick Fix: Reviving a Soft Crust

If your crust has already softened, you can bring back some crispness. Place the loaf directly on your oven rack at 375°F (190°C) for 5-8 minutes. This drives moisture out of the crust without significantly affecting the interior.

For sliced bread, toast individual slices. It's not the same as a fresh crispy crust, but it's delicious in its own way.

## The Bottom Line

A crispy sourdough crust is a battle against moisture—from inside the bread and from the environment. Bake thoroughly, cool completely in a dry space, and store in breathable materials. Follow these principles, and you'll be rewarded with that satisfying crunch for days after baking.

There's nothing quite like the sound of a knife breaking through a truly crispy sourdough crust. Once you nail these techniques, you'll never settle for soft again.
