# Sourdough Hydration Explained

Walk into any sourdough conversation and you'll immediately hear people throwing around numbers: "I bake at 75%," "this is a high-hydration dough," "what's your hydration?" It sounds technical. It isn't. Here's everything you need to know.

---

## What Is Hydration?

Hydration is simply the ratio of water to flour in your dough, expressed as a percentage.

**The formula:**
> Hydration % = (Weight of Water ÷ Weight of Flour) × 100

**Example:** If you use 325g of water and 450g of flour:
> 325 ÷ 450 × 100 = **72% hydration**

That's it. That's the whole thing.

---

## What Is Baker's Math?

Baker's math (or baker's percentages) treats flour as 100% and expresses every other ingredient as a percentage of the flour weight. This makes scaling recipes effortless.

In a standard sourdough formula:
| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's % |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| Bread flour | 450g | 100% |
| Water | 325g | 72% |
| Starter | 90g | 20% |
| Salt | 9g | 2% |

If you want to make two loaves, just double everything. The percentages stay the same.

---

## How Does Hydration Affect Your Bread?

Hydration changes everything about how the dough feels, behaves, and bakes.

### Lower Hydration (60–70%)
- Dough is stiffer and easier to handle
- Easier to shape — holds its form well
- Tighter crumb (fewer large holes)
- Slightly thicker crust
- Great for beginners

### Medium Hydration (70–78%)
- Sweet spot for most home bakers
- Dough is soft but manageable
- Good balance of open crumb and structure
- Classic country loaf territory

### High Hydration (78–85%+)
- Very wet, slack dough — hard to handle without experience
- Open, hole-y crumb (the kind you see on Instagram)
- Thinner, crispier crust
- Requires strong gluten development and technique
- **Not recommended until you've baked a dozen loaves**

---

## Why Do People Chase High Hydration?

The open crumb. Those dramatic, irregular holes in artisan bread are caused by a high-hydration dough with well-developed gluten trapping steam during baking. It's beautiful and it tastes great.

But here's the truth: **high hydration doesn't automatically equal better bread.** A well-made 72% loaf beats a poorly executed 80% loaf every single time. Start lower, master the technique, then add water.

---

## What Hydration Should You Start With?

**65–70%** for your first few loaves. This gives you a workable dough that won't stick to everything and will still produce a beautiful result.

Our classic country sourdough recipe uses **72%** — a great starting point that produces an open crumb without being unmanageable.

---

## How Flour Affects Hydration

Here's something nobody tells beginners: the same hydration percentage behaves differently depending on the flour you use.

**Bread flour** (12–14% protein) absorbs more water than all-purpose — you can handle higher hydration with it.

**All-purpose flour** (10–12% protein) is more delicate. At 75% it may feel unmanageable where bread flour handles it fine.

**Whole wheat and rye** absorb significantly more water. If you add whole wheat, you'll often need to increase hydration by 5% just to get the same feel.

**Aged flour** absorbs more water than fresh flour. Keep this in mind when recipes don't behave as expected.

---

## How to Adjust Hydration

Changing hydration is simple: adjust the water weight while keeping flour constant.

To take a recipe from 72% to 75%:
- Keep flour at 450g
- Change water: 450 × 0.75 = **337.5g** (up from 325g)

Make small adjustments — 2–3% at a time — and note the results. Your hands will quickly develop an instinct for what "right" feels like.

---

## The Hydration of Your Starter

Your starter is also a dough — typically maintained at 100% hydration (equal parts flour and water by weight). This factors into your final dough's total hydration.

If you're being precise, the total formula hydration includes the water in your starter. For most home bakers, this level of precision isn't necessary — the recipe percentages are calculated to account for it.

---

## A Simple Rule

**When in doubt, go lower.** A stiff dough that you can shape confidently will always beat a wet dough that's collapsing on your counter. As you bake more, you'll naturally push the hydration higher — your hands will tell you when you're ready.

The best hydration is the one that produces bread you're proud of.
