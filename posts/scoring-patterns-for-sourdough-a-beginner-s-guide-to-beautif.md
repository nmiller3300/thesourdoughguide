# Scoring Patterns for Sourdough — A Beginner's Guide to Beautiful, Functional Cuts

That dramatic slash across a bakery loaf isn't just decoration. Scoring is functional first, beautiful second — and understanding why we score will transform how your bread bakes.

When your loaf hits a hot oven, the yeast and bacteria go into overdrive, producing a final burst of gas. This is oven spring, and your dough needs somewhere to expand. Without a deliberate weak point, the bread will tear unpredictably — usually at the bottom or sides, creating a dense, misshapen loaf.

Scoring gives you control. You decide where the bread opens, how it rises, and ultimately how it looks.

## The Science Behind the Slash

Think of your shaped dough as a balloon with a tight skin. The surface tension you built during shaping holds everything together, but that same tension fights against expansion. A well-placed score creates a controlled release point.

Deep scores (about 1/4 to 1/2 inch) create dramatic ears — that beautiful flap of crust that lifts and caramelizes. Shallow scores spread wider and flatter. The angle of your blade matters too: a nearly vertical cut opens symmetrically, while a 30-45 degree angle undercuts the dough, creating that coveted ear.

## Essential Scoring Patterns for Beginners

### The Single Slash
This is where everyone should start. One confident cut down the center of a batard, angled at about 30 degrees, running nearly the full length of the loaf. This classic pattern directs all the oven spring to one dramatic opening.

**How to do it:** Hold your lame at a 30-degree angle to the dough surface. Start about 1 inch from one end, and in one swift motion, draw the blade through to about 1 inch from the other end. Aim for 1/4 to 1/2 inch depth.

### The Cross
Perfect for boules, this pattern creates four quadrants that expand evenly. It's forgiving for beginners because the symmetry balances the oven spring.

**How to do it:** Make two cuts that intersect in the center, each about 4-5 inches long. Keep your blade more vertical (about 90 degrees) for an open, rustic look.

### The Square or Box
Another excellent boule pattern. Four cuts forming a square on top create a beautiful crown effect as the center rises.

**How to do it:** Score four lines forming a square, about 1-2 inches from the edge of your boule. Each cut should be about 3-4 inches long and connect at the corners.

### The Crescent
A curved version of the single slash, this works beautifully on both boules and batards. The curve follows the natural round shape of the loaf.

**How to do it:** Start at one side and sweep in a gentle arc across the top, maintaining that 30-degree angle throughout.

## Common Scoring Mistakes

### Scoring Too Shallow
If your cuts barely break the surface, they'll seal back up almost immediately. The dough will find another weak point — usually an ugly blowout at the bottom. Commit to your cuts. You want to see the lighter, wetter dough beneath the skin.

### Scoring Too Deep
Going past 1/2 inch can deflate your dough significantly and compromise structure. You're cutting through the surface tension, not carving a canyon.

### Hesitating Mid-Cut
A jagged, stop-and-start score creates multiple weak points that open unevenly. The blade drags, the dough tears, and you get a ragged mess. One confident motion, always.

### Using a Dull Blade
A dull lame (or knife) drags through the dough instead of slicing cleanly. This pulls and deflates rather than cuts. Replace razor blades every 3-5 bakes, or after any bake where the blade dragged.

### Scoring Warm Dough
Dough straight from bulk fermentation is soft and sticky — nearly impossible to score cleanly. Always score after cold retarding, when the dough is firm and the surface is taut. If you're baking same-day, even 20 minutes in the freezer helps firm things up.

## Tips for Better Scores Every Time

1. **Wet or oil your blade** — A light coating of water or neutral oil helps the blade glide without sticking.

2. **Work quickly** — From the moment your dough leaves the cold banneton, the clock is ticking. Score within 30-60 seconds and get it in the oven.

3. **Score at an angle for ears** — That 30-degree undercut is the secret to dramatic ears. Vertical cuts look different — beautiful, but flatter.

4. **Practice on a floured countertop** — Before you score real dough, practice your motion and angle on a floured surface. Muscle memory matters.

5. **Start simple** — Master the single slash before attempting wheat stalks or intricate designs. Function before beauty.

## FAQ

### Can I use a regular knife instead of a lame?
Yes, but it's harder. A very sharp serrated knife works in a pinch, as does a new single-edge razor blade held carefully. The curved blade of a proper lame is designed for the slashing motion and angle that creates ears. If you're serious about sourdough, a lame is a worthwhile $10-15 investment.

### Why did my score seal back up and not open?
Three likely causes: your cut was too shallow, your dough was overproofed (not enough energy left for oven spring), or you lacked sufficient steam in the first phase of baking. Steam keeps the surface flexible long enough for the score to open. Make sure you're getting plenty of steam for the first 15-20 minutes.

### Do I need to score if I'm baking in a Dutch oven?
Technically, unscored bread will still bake — it'll just tear somewhere unpredictable. Scoring gives you control over your loaf's final shape and appearance. Even a simple cross or single slash makes a significant difference in how your bread looks and rises.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Score with purpose** — Every cut should be 1/4 to 1/2 inch deep, made with one confident motion
- **Angle matters** — 30 degrees for ears, 90 degrees for open, rustic expansion
- **Cold dough scores best** — Work straight from the fridge for clean cuts
- **Start with one pattern** — Master the single slash before moving to complex designs
- **Replace blades often** — A sharp blade is non-negotiable for clean scores

Scoring is where science meets art in sourdough baking. Start with function — a single, confident slash that lets your bread bloom in the oven. As your skills grow, your patterns can too. But even the most elaborate wheat stalk design follows the same basic principles: sharp blade, cold dough, confident motion, proper depth.

Your bread will thank you with a beautiful ear and the kind of oven spring that makes all those hours of fermentation worth it.
