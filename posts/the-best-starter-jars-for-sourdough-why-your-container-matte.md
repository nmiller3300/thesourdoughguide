Your sourdough starter is a living thing. It breathes, eats, and grows—and where it lives matters more than most bakers realize.

I've kept starters in everything from repurposed pickle jars to expensive purpose-built vessels. After years of daily feeding, I've learned that the right container doesn't just make maintenance easier—it actually helps you become a better baker by giving you clear visual feedback about your starter's health.

Let's find the perfect home for your culture.

## What Makes a Great Starter Jar

Before we talk brands, let's talk features. A good starter jar needs to do several things well:

**Straight sides** are essential. Tapered jars—the kind pasta sauce comes in—make it nearly impossible to track rise accurately. When your starter doubles in a jar that narrows at the top, the rise looks smaller than it actually is. Straight sides give you honest feedback.

**Wide mouth** matters for practical reasons. You need to get a spoon or spatula in there to stir and scrape down the sides. A narrow opening means sticky hands and missed bits of flour.

**Clear material** lets you observe bubble structure and rise from all angles. You're looking for those beautiful honeycomb bubbles throughout, not just on top.

**Appropriate size** gives your starter room to grow without overflowing. If you maintain 100g of starter (50g starter + 25g flour + 25g water), you want a jar that holds at least 500ml to accommodate a healthy triple rise.

**Easy to clean** might seem minor until you're scraping dried starter cement off textured glass at 6 AM.

## The Lid Question

This trips up a lot of new bakers: **your starter jar should never be airtight during active fermentation.**

Your starter produces carbon dioxide constantly. A sealed container builds pressure, which can crack glass or create a messy explosion when you open it. More importantly, some bakers believe a completely anaerobic environment shifts fermentation in undesirable ways.

You have several options:
- Loose-fitting lid (just set it on top without screwing down)
- Coffee filter or cloth secured with a rubber band
- Lid with a small hole drilled in it
- Purpose-built lids with one-way valves

I use a loose lid for daily maintenance—it keeps dust and fruit flies out while letting gas escape.

## Glass vs. Plastic

**Glass** is my preference for several reasons. It doesn't absorb odors or stain, it's easy to sterilize if you ever need to, and it stays put on the counter (lightweight plastic jars can tip when you're stirring a stiff starter). Glass also doesn't scratch, so you can scrape it clean without worrying about creating hiding spots for bacteria.

**Food-grade plastic** works fine if weight or breakage concerns you. Look for clear plastic without BPA, and plan to replace it yearly as scratches accumulate. Some bakers prefer plastic for refrigerator storage since it won't crack if your starter freezes against the back wall.

## Marks and Measurements

Here's where starter-specific jars really shine: measurement marks.

You can absolutely rubber-band a regular jar after each feeding to track rise. I did this for years. But printed measurements that stay put—showing both volume and rise percentage—eliminate guesswork and give you consistent data over time.

If you're using a plain jar, a piece of tape or a dry-erase marker works well. Mark the level after feeding so you can see exactly how much your starter has grown.

## My Recommendations

### Budget: Weck 743 Jar (~$5-7)

The Weck 743 (3/4 liter, about 750ml) is the workhorse of the sourdough world, and for good reason. Perfectly straight sides, wide mouth, thick glass that survives years of daily use. The included glass lid sits loosely enough to vent but keeps debris out.

No measurement marks, so you'll need to add your own with tape or a rubber band. But at this price, you can keep several on hand—one for your main starter, one for experiments, one as a backup.

**Pros:** Inexpensive, durable, perfect shape, widely available, attractive on the counter
**Cons:** No measurement marks, lid can slide off if bumped

### Mid-Range: Ball Wide Mouth Pint-and-a-Half (~$10-12 for a case)

The classic Mason jar gets the job done reliably. The pint-and-a-half size (24 oz/710ml) fits most starter maintenance routines perfectly. Measurement marks on the side help with tracking, though they're measuring volume, not rise percentage.

The two-piece lid system works well for starters—just use the ring without screwing it tight, or use just the flat lid set on top.

**Pros:** Extremely durable, replacement lids cheap and everywhere, volume marks included, fits standard fermentation lids
**Cons:** Slight shoulder below the mouth complicates stirring, marks can wear off over time

### Premium: Sourhouse Goldilocks Jar (~$25-30)

This is the only jar I know of designed specifically for sourdough starters, and it shows. Straight sides, wide mouth, measurement marks showing both volume and rise percentage (50%, 100%, 150%, etc.), and a custom lid with a one-way valve that vents CO2 automatically.

The glass is thick, the silicone seal is removable for cleaning, and the whole thing looks sharp on the counter. Is it necessary? No. Is it the best user experience for tracking starter health? Absolutely.

**Pros:** Purpose-built design, excellent rise tracking, vented lid, premium feel
**Cons:** Pricey for a jar, proprietary lid harder to replace, 550ml capacity may limit larger feeding ratios

## My Setup

I keep my active starter in a Weck 743 on the counter with a rubber band marking the post-feed level. Simple, cheap, effective. My backup starter lives in a Mason jar in the fridge.

If I were starting over and didn't mind spending more, I'd probably get the Sourhouse jar just for the clear rise percentage marks—it removes all ambiguity about whether your starter is ready to bake.

## A Note on Multiple Jars

However you go, own at least two jars. Transfer your starter to a clean jar every few feedings—this prevents the crusty buildup around the rim that can harbor mold or off-flavors. While one jar holds your starter, the other soaks in warm water, making cleanup effortless.

## The Bottom Line

Your starter will survive in almost any container that's clean, food-safe, and not airtight. But the right jar makes daily maintenance more pleasant and—more importantly—gives you accurate visual information about fermentation.

Start with a Weck jar or Mason jar. If you find yourself obsessing over rise percentages (welcome to the club), upgrade to something with proper markings. Your starter doesn't care about aesthetics, but you might—and there's nothing wrong with wanting a jar that looks as good as it functions.

Now go feed your starter. It's probably hungry.
