Every great sourdough loaf starts with a simple truth: flour matters more than almost anything else in your bread.

You can nail your fermentation timing, perfect your shaping, and score like an artist — but if you're using the wrong flour, your bread will never reach its potential. The good news? Understanding flour isn't complicated once you know what to look for.

Let's break it down so you can confidently choose the right flour for any sourdough you want to bake.

## What Makes Flour Different?

Flour isn't just ground wheat. Different flours have different **protein content**, and that protein (specifically gluten-forming proteins called glutenin and gliadin) determines how your dough behaves and how your bread turns out.

**Higher protein = stronger gluten = more structure and chew.**

**Lower protein = weaker gluten = more tender crumb.**

Here's a quick reference:

| Flour Type | Protein Content | Best For |
|------------|-----------------|----------|
| Bread flour | 11.5–13% | Classic sourdough loaves |
| All-purpose flour | 9.5–11.5% | Softer breads, discard recipes |
| Whole wheat flour | 13–14% | Hearty, nutty loaves |
| High-extraction flour | 11–13% | Rustic breads with more flavor |
| Tipo 00 flour | 11–12.5% | Pizza, focaccia |
| Rye flour | 8–10% | Flavor addition, dense breads |

## The Best Flour for Beginners

If you're just starting out, reach for **bread flour**.

Bread flour's higher protein content (typically 11.5–13%) gives you a forgiving dough that holds its shape, develops good structure, and produces that open, airy crumb sourdough lovers chase.

My go-to recommendation: **King Arthur Bread Flour** at 12.7% protein. It's consistent, widely available, and performs beautifully batch after batch.

Other excellent options:
- Bob's Red Mill Artisan Bread Flour (12.5%)
- Central Milling Organic Artisan Bread Flour (11.5%)
- Any unbleached bread flour from a reputable mill

## Can You Use All-Purpose Flour?

Absolutely — with some adjustments.

All-purpose flour has lower protein (9.5–11.5%), so your dough will be softer and more extensible. You might notice:
- A slightly stickier dough
- Less dramatic oven spring
- A tighter, more tender crumb

This isn't bad — it's just different. All-purpose works beautifully for sandwich loaves, focaccia, and any bread where you want a softer texture.

**Pro tip:** If all-purpose is what you have, use it. Don't let flour stop you from baking. You can always upgrade later.

## Adding Whole Grains

Whole wheat, rye, spelt, and other whole grain flours add incredible flavor and nutrition. But they behave differently than white flour.

Whole grain flours contain the bran and germ, which:
- Absorb more water (increase hydration by 5–10%)
- Cut through gluten strands (weakening structure)
- Ferment faster (more food for wild yeast)

**Start small.** Replace 10–20% of your bread flour with whole wheat or rye. As you gain experience, you can increase that percentage.

A reliable starting formula:
- 400g bread flour
- 100g whole wheat flour
- 375g water (75% hydration)
- 100g active starter
- 10g salt

This gives you whole grain flavor without sacrificing rise or structure.

## What About Bleached Flour?

Avoid it for sourdough.

Bleaching chemically ages flour and can affect gluten development and fermentation. Unbleached flour performs better and tastes better. The price difference is minimal — always choose unbleached.

## Common Flour Mistakes

**Mistake #1: Using old flour.**
Flour is a fresh product. Whole grain flours especially can go rancid within a few months. Store flour in a cool, dark place and use it within 3–6 months. If it smells musty or off, replace it.

**Mistake #2: Not knowing your flour's protein content.**
Protein content varies dramatically between brands. That generic store-brand all-purpose might be 9% protein while King Arthur's is 11.7%. Check the nutrition label — divide protein grams by serving size grams to get the percentage.

**Mistake #3: Switching flours mid-recipe without adjusting.**
Every flour absorbs water differently. If you switch from bread flour to whole wheat without adding more water, you'll end up with a stiff, dry dough. Always adjust hydration when changing flours.

**Mistake #4: Starting with 100% whole grain.**
Whole grain doughs are trickier to handle and ferment faster. Master bread flour first, then gradually incorporate whole grains.

## Flour for Your Starter

Your starter doesn't need fancy flour. Many bakers maintain healthy starters on:
- Unbleached all-purpose flour
- A 50/50 mix of all-purpose and whole wheat
- Whole rye flour (ferments vigorously)

Whole grain flours give your starter more nutrients and often result in more active fermentation. A tablespoon of rye flour in each feeding can really wake up a sluggish starter.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I mix different flours in one loaf?**

Yes! This is actually ideal. Most artisan bakers blend flours to get the perfect balance of strength, flavor, and texture. A classic approach is 80–90% bread flour with 10–20% whole wheat or rye for added complexity. Experiment to find your favorite blend.

**Q: Why does my dough feel different with a new bag of flour?**

Flour is an agricultural product that varies with harvest, weather, and storage conditions. Even the same brand can feel slightly different batch to batch. This is normal — adjust your water slightly if needed, and trust your hands more than exact numbers.

**Q: Is expensive flour worth it?**

Sometimes. Freshly milled flour from quality wheat genuinely tastes better and often performs better. But you don't need $15 flour to make excellent bread. A good mid-range bread flour like King Arthur will serve you beautifully for years. Invest in premium flour once you've built your skills and want to taste the difference.

## Practical Takeaways

1. **Start with bread flour** (11.5–13% protein) for the most forgiving, best-performing dough.

2. **Always use unbleached** — bleached flour affects fermentation and flavor.

3. **Check protein content** on the nutrition label when trying new brands.

4. **Add whole grains gradually** — start at 10–20% and increase as you gain confidence.

5. **Store flour properly** — cool, dark, and use within 3–6 months, especially whole grains.

6. **Adjust water when changing flours** — whole grains need more hydration, lower-protein flours need less.

The flour you choose shapes your bread's flavor, texture, and rise. But don't overthink it. Grab a bag of quality bread flour, start baking, and let experience teach you the rest. Your hands will learn what your flour needs faster than any article can explain.

Now go make some bread.
