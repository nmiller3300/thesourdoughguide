You scored your loaf beautifully. You slid it into a preheated Dutch oven with confidence. And then, somehow, your bread decided to ignore your careful cuts and burst open on the side instead—a jagged, unplanned tear that makes your loaf look like it's trying to escape itself.

This is called a side blowout, and while it's one of the most frustrating sourdough problems, it's also one of the most fixable. Once you understand why it happens, you can prevent it almost entirely.

## Why Side Blowouts Happen

During oven spring, the gases inside your dough expand rapidly. That expansion needs somewhere to go. Your score marks are supposed to provide a controlled release point—a deliberate weak spot where the dough can open gracefully.

When your loaf cracks on the side instead, it means the dough found an easier escape route than your score. Something about your shaping, scoring, or proofing created a weakness you didn't intend, or made your score ineffective.

Let's break down the specific causes and exactly how to address each one.

---

## Cause 1: Scoring Isn't Deep Enough

### Why This Happens

If your score is too shallow, it won't create enough of a weak point to direct the expansion. The skin of the dough—especially after cold retarding—can be surprisingly strong. A timid cut might look fine going in, but under the pressure of oven spring, the dough will find the path of least resistance elsewhere.

### How to Fix It

Score with confidence. For a standard batard or boule, you want your blade to cut through the outer skin and into the dough beneath—typically 1/4 to 1/2 inch deep, depending on your loaf size and hydration. You should see the cut open slightly as soon as you make it.

Hold your lame at a 30-45 degree angle to create an "ear," and move swiftly. Hesitation leads to dragging, which seals the cut rather than opening it.

### How to Prevent It

Practice your scoring motion before touching the dough. Use a sharp blade—dull lames drag and compress rather than slice. If you're using a razor blade in a lame, replace it every 3-4 bakes. The difference in sharpness is dramatic.

---

## Cause 2: Weak Shaping Left Gaps in the Structure

### Why This Happens

During shaping, you're creating surface tension—a taut outer skin that holds the loaf together. If your shaping is loose, or if you leave seams improperly sealed, those weak spots become the path of least resistance.

The side of a boule or the long seam of a batard are common blowout zones when shaping isn't tight enough.

### How to Fix It

Reshape if you catch it before proofing. If your dough feels slack or looks deflated after shaping, let it rest for 10 minutes, then reshape with more intention. Focus on creating tension across the entire surface, not just the top.

For a boule, use the bench scraper to drag the dough toward you repeatedly, tightening the bottom while creating surface tension on top. For a batard, make sure each fold during shaping overlaps properly and that you seal the seam with the heel of your hand.

### How to Prevent It

Don't rush shaping. This is where your loaf's structure is determined. A well-shaped loaf holds its form and directs expansion toward your score. Watch your seam placement too—it should be centered on the bottom, not creeping up the side where it can become a blowout point.

---

## Cause 3: Overproofing Weakened the Dough Structure

### Why This Happens

Overproofed dough has exhausted much of its structural integrity. The gluten network is stretched thin, and the dough doesn't have the strength to expand in a controlled way. Instead of pushing through your score, it tears wherever the structure is weakest.

Overproofed loaves often have multiple small tears rather than one dramatic blowout, though side cracks are common.

### How to Fix It

If you suspect overproofing, bake anyway—you'll still get edible bread. But note your timing for next time. The poke test is your friend here: properly proofed dough springs back slowly but doesn't fill in completely. Overproofed dough barely springs back at all.

### How to Prevent It

Track your proofing times and temperatures. Room temperature proofing for a standard loaf is typically 3-5 hours depending on your environment. Cold retarding in the refrigerator at 38-40°F (3-4°C) can go 8-16 hours for most recipes.

When in doubt, err on the side of slight underproofing. You can recover from underproofing with good oven spring. Overproofing is much harder to compensate for.

---

## Cause 4: Dough Skinned Over During Proofing

### Why This Happens

If the surface of your dough dries out during proofing, it forms a skin that's more resistant than the rest of the dough. This dry layer won't stretch with the expansion underneath. Your score might cut through the skin, but if the rest of the surface is too dry, the pressure will find another way out—usually the sides or bottom where moisture was retained.

### How to Fix It

If you notice a skin has formed, you can gently mist the surface with water before scoring. This won't fully solve the problem, but it can help. Score more deeply than usual to cut through the dried layer.

### How to Prevent It

Cover your dough properly during proofing. A damp towel, plastic wrap, or a shower cap over your banneton works well. For cold retarding, seal the banneton in a plastic bag or use a tight-fitting cover.

The ideal proofing environment has humidity. If your refrigerator is particularly dry, consider placing a damp towel in the bag with your banneton.

---

## Cause 5: Dutch Oven Wasn't Hot Enough

### Why This Happens

A properly preheated Dutch oven—450-500°F (230-260°C)—gives your dough an immediate burst of heat that triggers rapid oven spring. This fast expansion pushes through your score before the crust sets.

If your Dutch oven isn't hot enough, the oven spring happens more slowly. The crust starts to set all over, including at your score marks. When the expansion finally builds enough pressure, it breaks through wherever it can.

### How to Fix It

Preheat longer. Most ovens need 45-60 minutes with the Dutch oven inside to fully saturate with heat. Use an oven thermometer to verify your temperature—many ovens run cooler than their settings indicate.

### How to Prevent It

Make preheating non-negotiable. Set a timer when you turn on the oven, and don't rush it. A screaming-hot Dutch oven makes an enormous difference in how your score opens.

---

## The Quick Checklist

Before your next bake, run through this:

- Is my blade sharp enough?
- Am I scoring at least 1/4 inch deep?
- Did I create proper surface tension during shaping?
- Is my seam centered on the bottom?
- Did I proof at the right temperature for the right time?
- Did I keep my dough covered to prevent skinning?
- Did I preheat my Dutch oven for at least 45 minutes?

Side blowouts are frustrating, but they're also a clear signal from your bread. Once you identify which variable caused the problem, the fix is usually straightforward. Your next loaf will open exactly where you want it to.
