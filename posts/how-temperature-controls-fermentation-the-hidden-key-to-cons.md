You followed the recipe exactly. Same flour, same hydration, same timing. But this time your dough over-fermented into a sticky puddle, while last week it barely rose at all.

What changed? Almost certainly, the temperature.

Temperature is the invisible hand guiding your sourdough. It controls how fast your starter activates, how long bulk fermentation takes, and ultimately whether your loaf turns out perfectly or ends in frustration. Once you understand this relationship, you'll stop blaming yourself for "bad" bakes and start making adjustments that actually work.

## Why Temperature Matters So Much

Sourdough fermentation is driven by wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria. These microorganisms are alive, and like all living things, they respond to their environment. Temperature is their on/off switch.

Here's what happens at different temperatures:

- **Below 50°F (10°C)**: Fermentation nearly stops. This is why we use the refrigerator to slow or pause dough.
- **50-65°F (10-18°C)**: Very slow fermentation. Useful for long, cold retards that develop flavor.
- **65-75°F (18-24°C)**: Moderate fermentation. Gives you more time and control.
- **75-82°F (24-28°C)**: The sweet spot for most sourdough. Active fermentation with good flavor development.
- **Above 85°F (29°C)**: Fast fermentation. Increased acidity. Higher risk of over-fermentation.
- **Above 95°F (35°C)**: Yeast activity declines. Bacteria dominate. Dough becomes overly sour and slack.

The difference between a 68°F kitchen and a 78°F kitchen can mean hours of difference in your bulk fermentation time. A recipe that says "bulk ferment for 4-5 hours" assumes a specific temperature range—usually around 75-78°F. If your kitchen is cooler, you'll need longer. If it's warmer, you'll need less time.

## The Three Places Temperature Matters Most

### 1. Your Starter

A starter kept at 70°F might take 12 hours to peak. The same starter at 80°F might peak in 6-8 hours. If you're feeding your starter and expecting it to be ready for baking at a certain time, you need to account for your kitchen temperature.

**Practical tip**: In a cold kitchen, keep your starter in the oven with just the light on (usually adds 5-10°F) or on top of your refrigerator where it's slightly warmer.

### 2. Your Dough During Bulk Fermentation

Bulk fermentation is where temperature has the biggest impact on your schedule. A 4-hour bulk at 78°F becomes a 6-7 hour bulk at 68°F. Neither is wrong—they're just different.

This is also where desired dough temperature (DDT) comes into play. Most sourdough recipes aim for a final dough temperature of 75-78°F after mixing. You achieve this by adjusting your water temperature.

**Simple DDT formula**: 
Water temperature = (DDT × 3) - Room temp - Flour temp - Friction factor

For home bakers without a friction factor from a mixer, a simplified approach works:

If your kitchen and flour are around 70°F and you want a 76°F dough, use water around 88-90°F. If your kitchen is 80°F, use water around 68-70°F.

### 3. Cold Retard (Overnight Proof)

After shaping, most bakers refrigerate their dough overnight. The cold slows fermentation dramatically, allowing flavor to develop while you sleep. Your refrigerator should be between 36-40°F for a proper retard.

Dough can stay in the fridge anywhere from 8 to 48 hours depending on how fermented it was when it went in. Less fermented dough can handle a longer retard; well-fermented dough needs a shorter one.

## Common Mistakes

**Ignoring ambient temperature when following recipes**
A recipe written in San Francisco (average kitchen: 65°F) will behave completely differently in Houston (average kitchen: 78°F). Always watch your dough, not just the clock.

**Using cold water in a cold kitchen**
This gives you a dough temperature in the low 60s, which can take 8+ hours to bulk ferment. Use warm water to compensate for a cool environment.

**Letting dough get too warm**
Above 82°F, fermentation accelerates rapidly and becomes harder to control. The dough may become overly acidic, which weakens gluten. If your kitchen runs hot, use ice-cold water and consider shorter bulk times.

**Assuming the refrigerator pauses fermentation completely**
Fermentation continues slowly in the fridge. A dough that was already well-fermented can over-proof overnight. Under-ferment slightly before a long cold retard.

**Not measuring dough temperature**
You don't need an expensive thermometer. A basic instant-read kitchen thermometer ($10-15) lets you check your dough after mixing. This one measurement tells you more than any recipe timer.

## Tools for Temperature Control

- **Instant-read thermometer**: Check dough, water, and starter temperature. Essential.
- **Proofing box**: Maintains consistent temperature. Great for serious bakers or cold climates. The Brød & Taylor model holds at 70-85°F.
- **Oven with light on**: Creates a warm spot around 75-80°F in most ovens. Free and effective.
- **Cooler with warm water bottles**: DIY proofing box. Place a jar of warm water next to your dough container.
- **Room thermometer**: Know your baseline so you can adjust recipes accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: My kitchen temperature varies from 65°F in morning to 75°F in afternoon. How do I handle this?**

Start your dough in the morning using slightly warmer water to compensate for the cool start. As the day warms up, fermentation will naturally accelerate. Check your dough more frequently in the afternoon. Alternatively, use your oven with the light on to maintain a more consistent environment throughout the process.

**Q: Can I speed up fermentation by making my dough warmer?**

Yes, but with caution. You can safely push dough temperature up to about 80-82°F for faster fermentation. Beyond that, you risk over-fermentation, excessive acidity, and weakened gluten structure. Speeding things up also means less flavor development. If time is short, a slightly warmer dough is fine—but don't exceed 82°F.

**Q: How do I know if my dough over-fermented because of temperature?**

Over-fermented dough looks puffy but feels loose and jiggly rather than domed and structured. It may have large irregular bubbles on the surface and smell very sour or almost alcoholic. When you try to shape it, it tears easily and won't hold tension. If this happens on a warm day when you used your usual timing, temperature was likely the culprit. Next time, shorten your bulk fermentation or use cooler water.

## Practical Takeaways

1. **Buy a thermometer**. Check your dough temperature after mixing. Aim for 75-78°F.

2. **Know your kitchen**. Put a thermometer where you ferment your dough. Note how it changes throughout the day and across seasons.

3. **Adjust water temperature**. This is your main lever. Cold kitchen? Warm water. Hot kitchen? Cold water.

4. **Watch the dough, not the clock**. A recipe's timing is a guideline based on assumptions about temperature. Your dough will tell you when it's ready—look for 50-75% rise and a domed, jiggly surface.

5. **Create a consistent environment**. Whether it's your oven with the light on, a proofing box, or a specific spot in your house, find a place where temperature is predictable.

Once you understand temperature, you'll stop having mysterious failures and start having repeatable successes. The same recipe in the same kitchen will finally give you the same results—because you've mastered the variable that was changing all along.
