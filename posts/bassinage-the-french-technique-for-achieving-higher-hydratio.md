There's a ceiling most bakers hit when working with high-hydration dough. At some point—usually around 75-78%—the dough becomes unmanageable. It sticks to everything, tears during shaping, and feels more like batter than bread dough.

Bassinage breaks through that ceiling.

This French technique involves holding back a portion of your water and adding it gradually after the gluten network has already formed. The result? You can push hydration to 80%, 85%, even 90% while maintaining a dough that's actually workable. And the bread? Open, glossy, with an incredibly tender crumb.

## The Science Behind Bassinage

Gluten development requires two things: water and mechanical action. But here's what most bakers don't realize—gluten actually develops more efficiently at *lower* hydration levels.

When flour and water first combine, gluten proteins (glutenin and gliadin) need to find each other, bond, and form the elastic network that gives bread its structure. In a very wet environment, these proteins are essentially floating farther apart, making it harder for them to connect. It's like trying to find your friend in a crowded stadium versus a small room.

By starting with less water (typically 65-70% hydration), you create ideal conditions for rapid, strong gluten development. Once that network is established—tight, elastic, and organized—you can then hydrate it further. The existing gluten structure absorbs the additional water like a sponge, becoming extensible and supple without losing its integrity.

This is fundamentally different from simply mixing all the water at once. A pre-formed gluten network *holds* water; a developing one *struggles* with it.

## When to Use Bassinage

Bassinage isn't necessary for every bake. Use it when:

- **You want to exceed 78% hydration** with white flour
- **You're working with weaker flours** that can't handle high hydration from the start
- **You want maximum extensibility** for an open, irregular crumb
- **Your flour has strong absorption** and you're not sure of its final water capacity
- **You're learning high-hydration technique** and want more control

For a standard 72-75% country loaf with strong bread flour, bassinage is optional. For a high-hydration ciabatta-style crumb or when pushing your flour's limits, it's essential.

## Step-by-Step Bassinage Method

### The Formula

For a final dough of 85% hydration:
- 500g bread flour (12-13% protein)
- 325g water (initial mix) — this is 65%
- 100g active levain (100% hydration)
- 10g salt
- 75g water (bassinage) — added later to reach 85%

### Phase 1: Initial Mix and Autolyse

1. Combine 500g flour with 325g water. Mix until no dry flour remains.
2. Cover and rest for 45-60 minutes.

At this hydration, your dough will feel almost stiff—that's intentional. During autolyse, enzymes begin breaking down starches and proteins start hydrating.

### Phase 2: Add Levain and Develop Gluten

1. Add your 100g levain to the autolysed dough.
2. Pinch and fold to incorporate, about 2-3 minutes.
3. Perform slap-and-fold (French kneading) for 4-6 minutes until the dough becomes smooth, pulls away from the counter cleanly, and passes a basic windowpane test.

The dough should feel strong and elastic but not yet fully extensible. This is your foundation.

### Phase 3: Add Salt with First Bassinage Water

1. Sprinkle 10g salt over the dough.
2. Add 25g of your reserved bassinage water.
3. Dimple the water into the dough with your fingertips.
4. Pinch, fold, and squeeze until fully incorporated (2-3 minutes).

The dough will initially feel slippery and broken—this is normal. Keep working it until it comes back together, smooth and cohesive.

### Phase 4: Remaining Bassinage (Two Additions)

**First addition (25g water):**
1. Wait 15-20 minutes after incorporating salt.
2. Add 25g water using the same dimpling technique.
3. Perform stretch-and-folds in the bowl, pulling the dough over itself repeatedly.
4. Work until the water is fully absorbed and the dough is smooth again (3-5 minutes).

**Final addition (25g water):**
1. Wait another 20-30 minutes.
2. Add remaining 25g water.
3. Use the same incorporation method.
4. The dough will be noticeably softer but should still hold together.

### Phase 5: Bulk Fermentation

Proceed with your standard bulk fermentation, performing 3-4 sets of stretch-and-folds or coil folds over the first 2 hours. The dough will feel alive—jiggly, extensible, but not slack. Total bulk typically runs 4-5 hours at 75-78°F (24-26°C).

## Reading the Dough at Each Stage

Successful bassinage requires paying attention:

**After initial mix:** Dough is firm, almost clay-like. Good.

**After gluten development:** Smooth, springs back when poked, passes a rough windowpane. Ready for water.

**After salt addition:** Brief moment of "breaking" followed by re-cohesion. If it doesn't come back together, you need more development before proceeding.

**After each bassinage addition:** Temporary slack feeling, then strength returns within 15-20 minutes. If it stays slack, wait longer before the next addition.

**End of bulk:** Dough is puffy, jiggly, very extensible but not tearing. It should dome slightly when scooped, not pool flat.

## Common Bassinage Mistakes

### Adding Water Too Quickly

The most frequent error. If you dump all the reserved water in at once, you'll overwhelm the gluten network. Small increments (20-30g at a time) give the structure time to absorb and reorganize.

### Insufficient Initial Gluten Development

If your gluten network is weak before bassinage begins, adding water will destroy it entirely. Take time during Phase 2 to build genuine strength. The dough should feel tight and elastic, not just "mixed."

### Not Waiting Between Additions

Patience matters. After each water addition, the gluten needs 15-30 minutes to relax, hydrate, and regain structure. Rushing leads to progressively weaker dough.

### Using Cold Water

Cold water shocks the gluten and slows absorption. Use water at room temperature or slightly warm (75-80°F / 24-27°C) for the bassinage additions.

### Skipping Bassinage for Whole Grains

Whole wheat and rye absorb water slowly due to bran interference. Bassinage can actually help here—let the white flour portion build structure first, then add water to properly hydrate the whole grain component.

## Adjusting for Your Flour

Different flours have different absorption capacities:

- **Standard bread flour (12% protein):** Handles 80-85% total hydration via bassinage
- **High-gluten flour (14%+ protein):** Can push to 88-90%
- **All-purpose (10-11% protein):** Limit to 75-78% total
- **Fresh-milled flour:** Often absorbs more; you may need *additional* bassinage water beyond your formula

Start conservative. You can always add more water; you can't take it away.

## The Payoff

Once you've mastered bassinage, you'll approach high-hydration baking differently. Instead of fighting sticky, unmanageable dough, you'll work with dough that's strong, extensible, and a genuine pleasure to handle.

The bread speaks for itself: dramatic oven spring, a crumb that's open and glossy, and a texture that's simultaneously airy and chewy. This is how professional bakers achieve those Instagram-worthy cross-sections—not through magic, but through technique.

Bassinage is simply working *with* gluten chemistry instead of against it. Once you understand the why, the how becomes intuitive.
