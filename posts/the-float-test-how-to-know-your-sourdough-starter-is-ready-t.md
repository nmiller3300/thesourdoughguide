You've been feeding your starter faithfully. It bubbles, it rises, it smells pleasantly tangy. But here's the question that haunts every new sourdough baker: *Is it actually ready to bake with?*

The float test answers that question in about three seconds.

## What Is the Float Test?

The float test is exactly what it sounds like. You drop a small spoonful of your active starter into a glass of room-temperature water. If it floats, your starter is ready to leaven bread. If it sinks, it needs more time—or another feeding.

This simple test tells you whether your starter has produced enough carbon dioxide gas (trapped in a matrix of gluten and starches) to be buoyant. That same gas-producing power is what will make your bread rise.

## How to Perform the Float Test

**What you need:**
- A glass or clear container with room-temperature water (about 70-75°F / 21-24°C)
- A spoon
- Your active starter

**The method:**

1. Fill a glass with room-temperature water—about 3-4 inches deep is plenty.

2. Gently scoop a small dollop of starter from the center of your jar, about the size of a teaspoon. Avoid scraping from the sides or bottom where it may have dried slightly.

3. Carefully drop (don't plop) the starter onto the surface of the water.

4. Watch for 5-10 seconds.

**Reading the results:**
- **Floats:** Your starter is active, aerated, and ready to use.
- **Sinks slowly:** Getting close, but not quite there. Give it another 30-60 minutes.
- **Sinks immediately:** Your starter either hasn't peaked yet or has already passed its peak and deflated.

## When to Perform the Float Test

Timing matters more than most beginners realize.

Your starter goes through a predictable cycle after feeding: it begins fermenting, produces gas, rises to a peak, and then eventually collapses as it runs out of food. The float test only works during the window when your starter is at or near its peak activity.

For most starters fed at a 1:1:1 ratio (equal parts starter, flour, and water by weight) at room temperature (70-75°F / 21-24°C), this peak happens somewhere between 4-8 hours after feeding. But your kitchen, your flour, and your starter's unique microbial community all influence this timing.

Start checking around 4 hours after feeding, then every 30-60 minutes until you learn your starter's rhythm.

## Why the Float Test Works

When your starter ferments actively, wild yeast produces carbon dioxide gas. This gas gets trapped in the elastic network created by gluten and the sticky starches in your flour. The result is a bubbly, airy mass that's literally lighter than water.

Think of it like a tiny life jacket. Enough trapped air, and your starter floats. Not enough gas—or gas that's already escaped because the starter has deflated—and it sinks.

## Common Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

**Using water that's too cold or too hot.** Extreme temperatures can affect the test's reliability. Cold water is denser and might give a false positive. Hot water can degas your starter sample. Stick to room temperature.

**Scooping from the edges.** The starter along the walls of your jar is often drier, denser, or exposed to air longer. Always take your sample from the fluffy center.

**Testing too early (or too late).** A starter that hasn't peaked yet will sink. A starter that peaked three hours ago and has collapsed will also sink. Observe your starter's rise and test when it's at or near its highest point.

**Relying only on the float test.** The float test is helpful, but it's not the only indicator. A starter that has doubled or tripled in size, shows a domed top full of bubbles, and smells pleasantly yeasty is almost certainly ready—even if you skip the float test entirely.

**Using a starter that's too thin.** Very liquid starters (100%+ hydration) may not trap gas as effectively and can fail the float test even when active. If you keep a thinner starter, rely more on visual cues like doubling and bubble activity.

## What If Your Starter Keeps Sinking?

Don't panic. A sinking starter usually means one of three things:

1. **It hasn't peaked yet.** Be patient. Check again in an hour.

2. **It already peaked and collapsed.** You missed the window. Feed it again and watch more closely.

3. **It's not strong enough yet.** Young starters (under 2-3 weeks old) often aren't vigorous enough to pass the float test consistently. Keep feeding daily, and it will get there.

If your starter is more than a month old and never passes the float test, evaluate your feeding routine. It may need more frequent feedings, a warmer spot, or a switch to a flour with more wild yeast (like whole wheat or rye).

## FAQ

**Can I bake with a starter that sinks?**

You can, but results will be unpredictable. A sinking starter may still have some leavening power, but your bread will likely be denser with less oven spring. For consistent results, wait until your starter passes the test.

**Does the float test work for levains too?**

Absolutely. If you build a separate levain for your recipe, the float test works the same way. Test it when it looks domed, bubbly, and has roughly doubled in size.

**What if my starter floats but my bread still comes out dense?**

The float test confirms your starter is active—but it doesn't guarantee perfect bread. Dense loaves often result from underproofing, overproofing, weak gluten development, or incorrect fermentation temperatures. A passing float test is just one piece of the puzzle.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Test from the center.** Scoop your sample from the fluffy middle of your starter, not the drier edges.

- **Use room-temperature water.** Around 70-75°F (21-24°C) gives the most reliable results.

- **Time your test.** Perform the float test when your starter looks peaked—domed, bubbly, and at its maximum height.

- **Don't rely on it alone.** Combine the float test with visual cues: has it doubled? Does it have a domed top? Does it smell yeasty and tangy?

- **Learn your starter's schedule.** After a few bakes, you'll know exactly when your starter peaks without needing to test every time.

The float test isn't magic. It's just a quick, reliable way to confirm what your eyes, nose, and experience will eventually tell you on their own. Use it as a training tool now, and eventually, you'll know your starter is ready before you even reach for the glass.
