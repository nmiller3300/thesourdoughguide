# Why Is My Sourdough Dense?

You pulled your loaf from the oven full of hope, tapped the bottom (hollow — that's good!), waited the agonizing hour to cut it, and... dense. Heavy. More like a brick than bread.

It happens to almost every beginner. Here are the seven most common causes, in rough order of likelihood.

---

## 1. Your Starter Wasn't Active Enough

This is the most common reason for dense sourdough, and the most underdiagnosed.

**How to tell:** Your starter doesn't double within 4–8 hours of feeding. When you drop a small spoonful in water, it sinks instead of floats. Your dough felt heavy and didn't rise much during bulk fermentation.

**The fix:** Only bake with starter at peak activity — when it has doubled in volume and is bubbly throughout. Feed it, wait until it peaks, then use it. If your starter hasn't peaked yet, wait. Using under-ripe starter is like trying to inflate a balloon with almost-empty lungs — it works a little, but not enough.

If your starter consistently struggles to double, it may need rehabilitation: feed it daily for a week with fresh flour, keep it somewhere warm (75–78°F), and try using whole wheat flour for a few feedings to boost yeast activity.

---

## 2. Under-Fermentation During Bulk

The dough didn't ferment long enough, so there wasn't enough gas to give the crumb structure.

**How to tell:** Your dough didn't increase 50–75% during bulk. It feels dense when you handle it. The cut surface of the baked loaf has almost no holes.

**The fix:** Trust the dough, not the clock. A recipe that says "4 hours bulk" was written for a specific kitchen temperature. If yours is cooler, you might need 7–8 hours. Look for the signs: 50–75% volume increase, jiggle when you shake the container, small bubbles visible on the sides.

---

## 3. Over-Fermentation

Counterintuitively, too much fermentation also produces dense bread. The gluten structure degrades and can't hold gas anymore.

**How to tell:** Your dough was extremely sticky and hard to shape. It spread sideways instead of holding a round shape. The baked loaf is flat and slightly gummy, often very sour.

**The fix:** Shorten your bulk fermentation next time. Use the poke test: poke the dough ½ inch — it should spring back slowly and incompletely. If it doesn't spring back at all, you've gone too far.

---

## 4. Weak Gluten Development

Gluten is the network that traps gas bubbles and gives bread its structure. Weak gluten means gas escapes instead of staying in the dough.

**How to tell:** Your dough tore easily during shaping. It didn't feel smooth and elastic. The finished loaf is dense with an uneven crumb.

**The fix:** Perform all your stretch-and-fold sets during bulk fermentation, and be consistent about the 30-minute intervals. Don't rush through them. Stretch further and fold more firmly. If you consistently struggle, try a brief autolyse (mix flour and water, rest 30–60 minutes before adding starter and salt) — this pre-develops gluten before fermentation even begins.

---

## 5. Shaping Problems

Poor shaping leads to weak structure that collapses during baking.

**How to tell:** Your loaf spread sideways in the oven and didn't spring upward. The crumb is dense at the edges but slightly more open in the center.

**The fix:** Build tension during shaping. When you shape a round loaf, you're not just forming a ball — you're creating surface tension that holds everything together. After pre-shaping, let the dough rest (bench rest) for 20–30 minutes uncovered before final shaping. It'll be much more cooperative. During final shaping, use firm, confident movements — tentative handling produces tentative results.

---

## 6. Oven Issues

Even perfect dough can turn dense if the baking environment is wrong.

**How to tell:** The crust is pale or soft. There's no "ear" from the score. The loaf didn't spring much in the oven.

**The fix:**
- Preheat your oven AND Dutch oven for at least 45 minutes at 500°F. The Dutch oven must be blazing hot.
- Make sure your oven temperature is accurate — many home ovens run 25–50°F lower than displayed. An oven thermometer is a $10 investment that pays for itself immediately.
- Bake from cold (straight from the fridge after cold proof) — this helps oven spring.
- Score deep enough — at least ½ inch at a 30–45° angle. This gives steam an escape route and allows the loaf to expand.

---

## 7. Cutting Too Soon

This one is maddening because the bread is actually fine — you just ruined it by being impatient.

**How to tell:** The crumb is gummy and seems underbaked, but when you test the loaf it sounds hollow and the crust is properly dark.

**The fix:** Wait at least 1 hour after baking before cutting. The interior continues cooking from residual heat after you remove it from the oven, and the crumb structure sets as it cools. Cutting early releases steam, collapses the structure, and gives you a gummy result even from a perfectly baked loaf.

It's genuinely hard to wait. Put it on a wire rack in another room if you have to. It's worth it.

---

## The Most Important Thing

Don't get discouraged by a dense loaf. Every experienced sourdough baker has a collection of bricks in their past. The difference between beginners and experienced bakers isn't that experienced bakers never make mistakes — it's that they diagnose what went wrong and adjust for next time.

Keep notes. Note your room temperature, how long bulk took, what the dough felt like before shaping. Over 5–6 bakes, patterns will emerge and your bread will steadily improve.

The dense loaf you're cutting into right now? Make it into incredible croutons, or toast it thick and butter it generously. Nothing is wasted in sourdough baking.
