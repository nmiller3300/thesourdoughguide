You slice into your loaf expecting sourdough perfection. The crust crackles. You see those coveted open holes. But then—disappointment. The crumb around those holes is dense, wet, and almost paste-like. Gummy. Undercooked. What went wrong?

This frustrating combination—dramatic holes paired with gummy texture—is actually one of the most misunderstood problems in sourdough baking. The good news? Once you understand what's happening, it's completely fixable.

## Understanding the Problem

Big holes and gummy crumb might seem like opposite problems, but they often share the same root causes. Those impressive air pockets aren't necessarily a sign of great bread—they can actually be a symptom of fermentation or handling issues that also create that wet, underbaked texture.

Let's break down each cause and exactly how to address it.

---

## Cause #1: Overfermentation

### Why This Happens

This is the most common culprit. When dough overferments, the gluten structure begins to break down. The network that should hold gas evenly throughout the loaf becomes weak and irregular. Gas migrates and collects into fewer, larger pockets instead of being distributed in a uniform, honeycomb pattern.

Simultaneously, overfermented dough loses its ability to set properly in the oven. The weakened gluten can't hold its structure as starches try to gelatinize. The result? Those big holes surrounded by dense, gummy crumb that never quite bakes through.

### How to Fix It

Shorten your bulk fermentation. If you're currently fermenting for 5-6 hours at room temperature (around 72°F/22°C), try reducing to 4-4.5 hours. Watch for a 50-75% rise in volume rather than waiting for the dough to double.

The poke test helps: press a floured finger about half an inch into the dough. It should spring back slowly but not completely. If the indent stays and the dough feels fragile or jiggly like a waterbed, you've gone too far.

### How to Prevent It

Use your dough temperature as your guide, not just time. Dough at 78°F will ferment significantly faster than dough at 72°F. Invest in a probe thermometer and aim for a final dough temperature between 75-78°F for predictable timing.

Set a timer to check your dough every 30 minutes during the last half of bulk fermentation. The difference between perfectly fermented and overfermented can be just 30-45 minutes.

## Cause #2: Insufficient Baking Time or Temperature

### Why This Happens

Even properly fermented dough can end up gummy if it's underbaked. Large holes make this worse because the thick walls of dough surrounding those cavities need more time to bake through than a uniform crumb would.

Many bakers pull their loaves when the crust looks golden, but the interior hasn't finished setting. The residual moisture stays trapped, creating that gummy, wet texture.

### How to Fix It

Extend your bake time and trust internal temperature over appearance. A fully baked sourdough should reach an internal temperature of 205-210°F (96-99°C). Use an instant-read thermometer inserted into the center of the loaf.

If you've been baking at 450°F (230°C) for 40 minutes total, try extending to 45-50 minutes. For the last 10-15 minutes, you can reduce the temperature to 425°F (218°C) to prevent the crust from burning while the interior finishes.

### How to Prevent It

Always verify with temperature, not just time or color. Different flour blends, hydration levels, and loaf sizes all affect baking time. A higher-hydration dough or a larger loaf will need longer than a standard recipe suggests.

After removing the lid from your Dutch oven, give the bread adequate time—at least 20-25 minutes—to finish baking and allow moisture to escape.

## Cause #3: Cutting Too Soon

### Why This Happens

This one breaks hearts. You've done everything right, but you slice into the loaf while it's still warm. The crumb hasn't finished setting.

Baking doesn't actually end when you pull the loaf from the oven. The interior continues cooking as residual heat redistributes, and excess moisture escapes as steam. Cut too early, and you interrupt this process. The crumb compresses under the knife, moisture stays trapped, and you get that gummy, undercooked texture—especially around those big holes.

### How to Fix It

Wait at least 2 hours before cutting. For larger loaves or high-hydration breads, wait 3-4 hours. The loaf should be completely cool to the touch, including the bottom.

If you've already cut too soon and discovered gummy crumb, you can salvage the loaf by slicing it and toasting the pieces thoroughly.

### How to Prevent It

Build the cooling time into your baking schedule. If you want fresh bread for dinner at 6 PM, plan to pull it from the oven by 3 PM at the latest. Better yet, bake the day before—sourdough flavor actually develops and improves overnight.

## Cause #4: Poor Shaping Creating Uneven Structure

### Why This Happens

Weak or inconsistent shaping allows gas to migrate into large pockets instead of staying evenly distributed. If you're not building enough surface tension during shaping, the dough can't hold a uniform structure. Gas collects where the dough is loosest, creating those dramatic holes while other areas stay dense and underdeveloped.

### How to Fix It

Focus on creating even tension across the entire surface of your loaf. During pre-shaping, use your bench scraper to drag the dough toward you, letting the friction of the counter create tension on the bottom. Let it rest 20-30 minutes, then shape firmly.

For the final shape, whether boule or batard, ensure you're sealing the seam completely and that tension is consistent all around—not just tight in one spot.

### How to Prevent It

Practice the "window pane" check before shaping. Stretch a small piece of dough thin enough to see light through. If it tears immediately, the gluten needs more development. If it stretches into a translucent membrane, you're ready to shape.

Don't skip the bench rest between pre-shape and final shape. Those 20-30 minutes allow the gluten to relax, making it easier to shape tightly without tearing.

## Cause #5: Too Much Starter or Very Active Starter

### Why This Happens

Using a high percentage of very active starter accelerates fermentation dramatically. The dough can overferment before you realize it, leading to the same gluten breakdown and irregular gas distribution described earlier.

A young, just-peaked starter is incredibly vigorous. If your recipe calls for 100g of starter and you're using it right at peak activity with a warm dough, fermentation can outpace your timeline.

### How to Fix It

Reduce your starter percentage or use it slightly past peak. Most recipes call for 15-20% starter (relative to flour weight). If you're using more, try scaling back to 10-15%.

Alternatively, use your starter when it's active but just past peak—still bubbly and domed, but starting to show slight deflation. It will still raise your bread but won't cause runaway fermentation.

### How to Prevent It

Keep notes on your starter's behavior. Know how long it takes to peak at your kitchen temperature. If it peaks in 4 hours, plan to feed it so it's ready when you need it—not hours before.

---

## The Bottom Line

Big holes with gummy crumb usually signals overfermentation, underbaking, or both. The fix almost always involves:

- Fermenting less (watch for 50-75% rise, not double)
- Baking longer (internal temp of 205-210°F)
- Waiting longer before cutting (minimum 2 hours)

Start with these adjustments, and those beautiful holes will finally come with the light, fully-baked crumb they deserve.
