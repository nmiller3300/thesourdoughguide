If you take away one piece of advice from everything I've learned about sourdough, it's this: **buy a kitchen scale**.

I know that sounds dramatic for a $15-$50 purchase, but hear me out. Volume measurements—cups and tablespoons—are wildly inconsistent. One cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 120g to 160g depending on how you scoop it, whether the flour is sifted, and even the humidity in your kitchen. That 40-gram difference? It's the gap between a perfectly hydrated dough and a sticky mess you can't shape.

Once you switch to weighing ingredients, your sourdough becomes *repeatable*. That incredible loaf you baked last Tuesday? You can make it again. And again. That's the real magic of a scale.

## What to Look for in a Sourdough Scale

Not all kitchen scales are created equal, and sourdough baking has some specific demands. Here's what actually matters:

### Capacity (At Least 5kg/11lb)

A full batch of sourdough dough can easily weigh 1.5-2kg, and you're placing that on top of a mixing bowl that might weigh another kilogram. If you ever want to double a recipe or weigh ingredients directly into your Dutch oven, you'll need headroom. I recommend **at least 5kg capacity**, with 7-10kg being ideal for serious bakers.

### Precision (1g Increments, Ideally 0.1g for Starters)

For most dough ingredients, 1-gram precision is fine. But when you're feeding a starter with 25g of flour and 25g of water, being off by 2-3 grams actually affects hydration. Some bakers keep a separate **pocket scale with 0.1g precision** just for starter feeding. It's not essential, but it's a nice upgrade once you're hooked.

### Tare Function

Every decent scale has this, but make sure it works quickly and reliably. You'll be taring constantly—after the bowl, after the flour, after the water. A sluggish tare button will drive you crazy.

### Response Time

Cheap scales can lag, showing the weight 2-3 seconds after you've added ingredients. When you're pouring water and trying to hit exactly 350g, that delay means constant overshooting. Better scales respond almost instantly.

### Platform Size

A tiny platform works fine for a small bowl, but larger mixing bowls can obscure the display or feel unstable. Look for platforms at least 5-6 inches across, or scales with pull-out displays.

### Auto-Off Override

This is the hidden frustration of cheap scales. You're mid-recipe, step away to answer the door, and your scale has shut off—losing your tared weight and your running total. The best scales either have long auto-off timers (5+ minutes) or let you disable the feature entirely.

## The Honest Trade-Offs

Let's be real about the compromises at each price point:

**Budget scales ($10-20)** work, but they cut corners. Expect slower response times, shorter auto-off windows, and less durability. The buttons wear out, the accuracy drifts over time, and plastic platforms scratch easily. If you're just starting out, they're fine—but plan to upgrade within a year or two.

**Mid-range scales ($25-50)** hit the sweet spot for most home bakers. You get faster response, better build quality, and features like longer auto-off or backlit displays. These last for years with normal use.

**Premium scales ($60-150)** are genuinely excellent, but you're paying for precision and durability you may not need. Professional bakers, recipe developers, and serious hobbyists will appreciate the upgrade. Everyone else might find it overkill.

## How I Actually Use My Scale

Here's my workflow for a typical bake:

1. **Bowl on scale, tare.** Add flour directly, note the weight.
2. **Tare again.** Add water until I hit my target hydration.
3. **Tare again.** Add salt and starter.
4. **Final check.** I weigh the total dough after mixing to confirm I haven't forgotten anything.

During fermentation, I sometimes weigh my starter jar to track activity—if it doubles, the weight distribution shifts and you can actually see the jar get lighter on one side. It's a neat trick.

For dividing dough, I weigh each piece rather than eyeballing. Two 750g loaves will bake more evenly than one 800g and one 700g.

## The Bottom Line

A kitchen scale isn't just "nice to have" for sourdough—it's the single most important tool for consistent results. The difference between a $15 scale and a $50 scale is real but not dramatic. Start with whatever fits your budget, use it for every bake, and upgrade when (if) you feel limited.

The scale you actually use is better than the perfect scale sitting in your cart. Get one, start weighing, and watch your sourdough transform from guesswork into craft.
