You lift the lid off your starter jar and instead of that pleasant, yeasty tang, you're hit with something that smells like nail polish remover. Sharp. Chemical. Almost eye-watering.

Don't panic. Your starter isn't ruined. That acetone smell is actually your starter communicating with you—it's telling you something specific about what's happening inside that jar, and once you understand the message, fixing it is straightforward.

## Why Does My Starter Smell Like Acetone?

That nail polish remover odor comes from acetone and other volatile compounds produced when your starter's bacterial community shifts into survival mode. Here's what's actually happening at the microbial level.

### Cause #1: Your Starter Is Hungry (Severely Underfed)

**Why this happens:**

Your sourdough starter is a living ecosystem of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria (LAB). When you feed it flour and water, these microorganisms feast on the starches and sugars, producing carbon dioxide, alcohol, and various acids.

But when food runs out—when you've gone too long between feedings—the yeast continues fermenting, but now it's stressed. Instead of producing the pleasant alcoholic and acidic byproducts of healthy fermentation, stressed yeast produces acetone and other harsh-smelling ketones. The bacterial balance also shifts, with acetic acid-producing bacteria dominating over the milder lactic acid bacteria.

Think of it like this: a well-fed starter smells like a bakery. A starving starter smells like a chemistry lab.

**How to fix it:**

1. Discard all but 25 grams of your starter
2. Feed with 50 grams flour and 50 grams water (1:2:2 ratio)
3. Let it sit at room temperature (70-75°F / 21-24°C) for 4-6 hours
4. Repeat this feeding twice daily for 2-3 days
5. The smell should improve noticeably after the first feeding and return to normal within 48-72 hours

**How to prevent it:**

Establish a consistent feeding schedule. At room temperature, feed every 12 hours. If you bake less frequently, store your starter in the refrigerator and feed it once weekly—but take it out 1-2 days before baking and resume twice-daily feedings to wake it up.

### Cause #2: Your Starter Is Too Warm

**Why this happens:**

Temperature dramatically affects fermentation speed and the balance between yeast and bacteria. When your starter sits in a warm environment—above 80°F (27°C)—fermentation accelerates significantly. Your starter burns through its food supply faster, and the bacterial populations that produce acetic acid (and its harsher-smelling relatives) thrive.

In summer months or if your starter lives near a warm appliance, it might be perfectly healthy at your regular feeding schedule one week, then suddenly smell like acetone the next because ambient temperature increased.

**How to fix it:**

1. Move your starter to a cooler location (68-75°F / 20-24°C is ideal)
2. Give it a generous feeding: 25 grams starter, 75 grams flour, 75 grams water
3. Monitor how quickly it rises and falls—in warmer conditions, you may need to feed more frequently

**How to prevent it:**

Find a consistent, moderate-temperature spot for your starter. Avoid locations near ovens, dishwashers, refrigerator vents, or sunny windows. If your kitchen runs warm, consider feeding with cooler water (around 65°F / 18°C) to slow fermentation, or switch to refrigerator storage with weekly feedings.

### Cause #3: Too Much Acetic Acid Fermentation

**Why this happens:**

Your starter contains two main types of lactic acid bacteria: homofermentative (producing mainly lactic acid) and heterofermentative (producing lactic acid, acetic acid, and CO2). When conditions favor the acetic acid producers—typically cooler temperatures combined with longer fermentation times, or a very stiff starter consistency—acetic acid builds up.

Extreme acetic acid accumulation creates that sharp, vinegary-to-acetone smell spectrum. The environment becomes increasingly acidic, which further stresses the yeast and compounds the problem.

**How to fix it:**

1. Discard down to 10-15 grams of starter (a smaller amount means less acidic carryover)
2. Feed with 50 grams flour and 50 grams water
3. Keep at a slightly warmer temperature: 75-78°F (24-26°C)
4. Feed again as soon as it peaks—don't let it collapse and sit

**How to prevent it:**

Maintain a 100% hydration starter (equal weights flour and water) rather than a stiff starter, and keep it at moderate room temperature. Lactic acid bacteria, which produce milder flavors, prefer slightly warmer conditions and more hydrated environments. Acetic acid bacteria prefer cooler temperatures and stiffer doughs.

### Cause #4: The Hooch Has Mixed Back In

**Why this happens:**

Hooch is the dark liquid that forms on top of a neglected starter. It's mostly alcohol and water, with concentrated acids and yes, those acetone-like compounds. When you stir it back into your starter rather than pouring it off, you're reintroducing all those harsh-smelling byproducts.

Some bakers routinely stir hooch back in to add sourness to their bread—but if hooch has accumulated substantially (more than a thin layer), it can overwhelm the starter with acidity and off-flavors.

**How to fix it:**

1. Pour off the hooch completely
2. Discard all but 20 grams of the starter beneath it
3. Feed with 60 grams flour and 60 grams water
4. Repeat twice daily until the smell normalizes (usually 1-2 days)

**How to prevent it:**

If you're storing your starter in the refrigerator and hooch develops, pour it off before feeding. A thin layer occasionally is normal and nothing to worry about—but if you're consistently seeing significant hooch, your starter needs more frequent feedings or a larger feeding ratio.

## What If the Smell Persists?

If you've been feeding consistently for 3-4 days with fresh flour and water and the acetone smell remains, try this reset protocol:

1. Take just 5 grams of your starter
2. Mix with 50 grams flour and 50 grams water
3. Feed every 8-12 hours at room temperature
4. Use whole grain rye or whole wheat flour for 2-3 feedings—the extra nutrients and wild yeast can help rebalance the ecosystem
5. Switch back to your regular flour once the smell improves

This aggressive dilution reduces the acidic load and gives the beneficial microorganisms a chance to reestablish dominance.

## Can You Still Bake With an Acetone-Smelling Starter?

Technically, yes—it's not dangerous. But I wouldn't recommend it. A stressed starter won't have the leavening power of a healthy one, and those off-flavors can carry through into your final bread, giving it harsh, unpleasant acidic notes rather than the complex tanginess we love in sourdough.

Take 2-3 days to rehabilitate your starter first. The bread will be infinitely better for it.

## The Takeaway

That acetone smell is simply your starter waving a red flag: *I'm hungry, I'm stressed, I need attention.* With consistent feedings, moderate temperatures, and a little patience, your starter will return to its pleasant, yeasty self within days.

And once you understand why it happened, you can prevent it from happening again. A well-fed starter at the right temperature shouldn't smell harsh—it should smell like possibility.
