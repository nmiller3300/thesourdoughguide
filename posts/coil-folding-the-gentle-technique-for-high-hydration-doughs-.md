When I first started baking high-hydration sourdough, stretch and folds felt like a wrestling match. The dough stuck to everything, tore at the edges, and left me questioning every choice I'd made. Then I learned coil folding, and everything changed.

Coil folding is a dough strengthening technique that works *with* wet, slack doughs rather than against them. It's gentler than stretch and fold, requires no flour or wet hands, and builds structure without degassing your carefully developed fermentation bubbles. Once you understand the mechanics, you'll reach for this technique constantly.

## The Science Behind Coil Folding

Gluten development in bread dough happens through two mechanisms: mixing and time. When you fold dough, you're physically aligning gluten strands and creating layers of organized structure. But here's what makes coil folding special — it works primarily through *tension* rather than *stretching*.

When you lift the dough from the center and let gravity pull the sides down, you're creating tension across the entire gluten network simultaneously. The dough stretches under its own weight, which is far gentler than manually pulling it. This matters because aggressive handling can tear the delicate gas bubbles that wild yeast has worked so hard to create.

The technique also excels at redistributing temperature and yeast activity throughout the dough mass. As you coil, cooler outer portions move inward, and warmer center portions move outward, creating more even fermentation.

For doughs above 75% hydration, coil folding becomes almost essential. The higher water content means a more extensible gluten network — great for open crumb, but challenging to handle. Traditional stretch and folds can overwork these doughs, leading to tearing and a dense final product.

## When to Use Coil Folding

Coil folding shines in specific situations:

**High-hydration doughs (75%+)**: The wetter your dough, the more coil folding makes sense. At 80% hydration and above, it becomes my primary folding method.

**Doughs with inclusions**: Adding cheese, olives, or nuts? Coil folding during the addition helps distribute ingredients evenly without deflating the dough.

**Mid-to-late bulk fermentation**: As fermentation progresses and gas bubbles develop, coil folding preserves more of that precious air than stretch and fold.

**When your dough feels fragile**: Some flour blends, especially those high in whole grain, create more delicate gluten networks. Coil folding treats them with appropriate gentleness.

## Step-by-Step Coil Folding Technique

**What you need:**
- Dough in a straight-sided container (clear is helpful for monitoring)
- Clean, dry hands
- A surface to work over (I use a silicone mat)

### The Process

**Step 1: Wet your hands lightly**
Just damp — not dripping. You want enough moisture to prevent sticking, but not so much that you're adding water to the dough.

**Step 2: Release the dough edges**
Run your fingers around the perimeter of the container to gently release any dough sticking to the sides.

**Step 3: Slide hands under the center**
Reach under the dough from opposite sides, meeting your fingers in the middle beneath the dough mass. Your hands should be roughly in the center third of the dough.

**Step 4: Lift straight up**
Raise the dough about 8-10 inches above the container. Let gravity do the work — the sides will stretch downward naturally. Don't rush this. Let the dough hang for 2-3 seconds.

**Step 5: Fold one end under**
As you lower the dough back toward the container, allow one end to fold underneath the center mass. Think of it like the dough is coiling onto itself — hence the name.

**Step 6: Fold the other end under**
Continue lowering until the opposite end also folds beneath the center.

**Step 7: Rotate and repeat**
Turn your container 90 degrees. Repeat steps 3-6 to fold in the perpendicular direction.

That's one complete set of coil folds — four folds total (two in each direction).

### Timing and Frequency

For a typical high-hydration loaf, I perform coil folds every 30-45 minutes during the first 2-3 hours of bulk fermentation:

- **First fold**: 30 minutes after mixing (or after autolyse incorporation)
- **Second fold**: 45 minutes later
- **Third fold**: 45 minutes later
- **Fourth fold**: 45 minutes later (if dough still feels slack)

You'll typically need 3-4 sets of folds for high-hydration doughs. Stop folding when the dough holds its shape, feels cohesive, and shows visible surface tension.

## Reading Your Dough

The real skill in coil folding is knowing when you've done enough. Watch for these signs:

**Ready for more folds:**
- Dough spreads flat within minutes of folding
- Feels slack and extensible
- Little visible surface tension

**Adequately developed:**
- Dough maintains a domed shape after folding
- Surface looks smooth and slightly taut
- Visible bubbles beneath the surface
- Dough feels lighter and more airy when lifted

**Over-developed (stop folding):**
- Dough resists stretching
- Feels tight and springs back quickly
- Surface looks stressed or uneven

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

### Lifting Too Fast
**The problem**: Yanking the dough upward tears the gluten network.
**The fix**: Lift slowly, taking 2-3 full seconds to raise the dough. Let gravity stretch it naturally.

### Not Lifting High Enough
**The problem**: Insufficient height means insufficient stretch, which means insufficient strengthening.
**The fix**: Aim for 8-10 inches of lift. The dough should stretch significantly before you lower it.

### Folding Too Often
**The problem**: Excessive folding creates a tight, dense crumb instead of the open structure you want.
**The fix**: Once the dough holds its shape and shows good surface tension, stop. More isn't better.

### Folding Too Late in Bulk
**The problem**: Folding a nearly-proofed dough degasses it significantly.
**The fix**: Complete your folds in the first half to two-thirds of bulk fermentation. The final portion should be hands-off.

### Using Too Much Water on Hands
**The problem**: Excess water creates a slick layer, making it hard to grip the dough center.
**The fix**: Damp hands only. If dough slips through your fingers, they're too wet.

### Inconsistent Timing
**The problem**: Folding at random intervals creates unpredictable results.
**The fix**: Set timers. Consistency in timing leads to consistency in results.

## Coil Fold vs. Stretch and Fold: When to Choose Each

Both techniques build gluten structure, but they excel in different situations:

| Situation | Best Choice |
|-----------|-------------|
| Hydration under 72% | Stretch and fold |
| Hydration 72-78% | Either works well |
| Hydration above 78% | Coil fold |
| Early bulk fermentation | Stretch and fold |
| Mid-to-late bulk | Coil fold |
| Adding inclusions | Coil fold |
| Very strong flour | Stretch and fold (fewer needed) |
| Delicate whole grain doughs | Coil fold |

Many experienced bakers, myself included, use both techniques in a single bake — stretch and folds early when the dough is tighter, transitioning to coil folds as it relaxes and fermentation progresses.

## A Sample Schedule for 78% Hydration Dough

Here's how I incorporate coil folding into a typical bake:

- **0:00** — Mix flour and water, begin autolyse
- **1:00** — Add levain and salt, pinch to incorporate
- **1:30** — First stretch and fold (dough is still relatively tight)
- **2:15** — Second stretch and fold
- **3:00** — First coil fold (dough has relaxed significantly)
- **3:45** — Second coil fold
- **4:30** — Third coil fold (assess if more are needed)
- **5:15** — Fourth coil fold if dough is still slack; otherwise, leave untouched
- **6:30-7:00** — End bulk fermentation, pre-shape

This schedule assumes 76-78°F ambient temperature. Adjust timing based on your kitchen conditions and dough behavior.

## The Transformation You'll See

When coil folding is working, the change in your dough is dramatic. What starts as a shaggy, slack mass transforms into something alive — billowy, domed, covered in visible bubbles, and holding its shape with quiet confidence.

The first time you successfully handle an 80% hydration dough using coil folds, you'll wonder why you ever fought with stretch and folds at high hydration. The technique feels natural because it is — you're simply letting gravity and time do the work, guiding rather than forcing.

Master coil folding, and you unlock a whole new range of breads. Those glossy, hole-filled crumb shots you've admired? They're within reach. That porridge bread you've been afraid to attempt? Entirely possible.

Start with your next bake. Your hands — and your crumb — will thank you.
